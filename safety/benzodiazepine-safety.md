# Benzodiazepine safety

> Educational harm-reduction content. In an emergency call **911**.

## What overdose looks like

A benzodiazepine overdose on its own usually looks like extreme sleepiness: slurred speech, confusion, stumbling, being very hard to wake, and slow reflexes. Breathing may slow. In older adults, people with lung disease or sleep apnea, and when other drugs are involved, breathing can stop.

## The danger of mixing

Benzodiazepines combined with **opioids** are involved in a large share of overdose deaths. Each drug slows breathing in a different way, and together the effect multiplies. In 2016 the FDA added its strongest warning to both opioid and benzodiazepine labels about this combination. **Alcohol** has the same multiplying effect, plus vomiting while unconscious. Gabapentinoids, sleep medicines, and other sedatives add risk too.

Counterfeit pills sold as Xanax often contain designer benzodiazepines like bromazolam, and sometimes fentanyl. If a person is unresponsive after taking a pill not from a pharmacy, **give naloxone**: it will not reverse the benzodiazepine, but it can reverse any opioid part and restore breathing.

## Blackouts

Benzodiazepines block the formation of new memories. People can walk, talk, text, drive, or have sex and later remember nothing. This leads to injuries, legal trouble, risky behavior, and repeat dosing because the person forgets they already took some. Flunitrazepam and other benzos have been used in drug-facilitated sexual assault.

## Withdrawal is dangerous

After weeks or more of daily use, the brain adapts. Stopping suddenly can cause severe anxiety, insomnia, tremor, sweating, sensitivity to light and sound, hallucinations, delirium, and **seizures that can be fatal**. This is one of the few drug withdrawals that can kill. Anyone who has been taking a benzodiazepine daily should talk to a doctor about a slow, supervised taper, often switching to a long-acting drug like diazepam and reducing gradually over weeks to months (the Ashton Manual approach). Some people experience protracted withdrawal symptoms for months.

## What to do in an emergency

1. Try to wake them; shout and rub the breastbone.
2. Call 911.
3. Give naloxone if any chance of opioids.
4. Support breathing; rescue breaths if trained.
5. Recovery position, on their side.
6. Stay with them.

Flumazenil can reverse benzodiazepines but is used only in hospitals because it can cause seizures in dependent people.

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, 24/7). See also [opioid overdose](opioid-overdose.md) and [naloxone](../narcan/naloxone.md).
