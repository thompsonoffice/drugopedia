# Inhalant safety

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice. No amounts, preparation, or sourcing. In an emergency in the US, call 911. Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222. SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357.

## Why this page exists

Inhalants include solvents (glue, gasoline, paint thinner), gases (butane, propane), aerosols (dusters, spray cans), nitrous oxide, and poppers (alkyl nitrites). They are cheap and found in every home, and young teens use them most. They can kill on the very first use.

## The biggest dangers

### Sudden sniffing death

Many solvents and propellants make the heart overly sensitive to adrenaline. A scare, being chased, or exertion can trigger a fatal heart rhythm within minutes.

### Suffocation and choking

Bags and masks cut off oxygen. Vomiting while intoxicated can block the airway.

### Freezing and burns

Gases straight from a can are extremely cold and can freeze the throat and lungs. Many inhalants are flammable.

### Poppers

Poppers drop blood pressure. With erectile dysfunction medicines this can cause collapse, heart attack or stroke. Swallowing the liquid can be fatal.

### Long-term damage

Solvents damage the brain, nerves, hearing, liver, kidneys and bone marrow. Nitrous oxide causes B12-related nerve damage.

## Warning signs

- Collapse, no breathing or no pulse.
- Chemical smell on breath or clothes, paint stains, slurred speech, confusion.
- Seizures.

## What to do

1. Call 911. Start CPR if they are not breathing normally.
2. Do not chase, startle or argue with them.
3. Move them to fresh air and remove any bag.

## For parents and caregivers

Signs of inhalant use include chemical smells, empty cans or rags, sores around the mouth, and sudden drop in school performance. Calm, early conversations help.
