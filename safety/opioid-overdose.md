# Opioid overdose: what it feels like, what it looks like, and what to do

> Educational harm-reduction content. If someone may be overdosing right now: **call 911 and give naloxone.**

## What an overdose feels like from the inside

Survivors almost always say the same thing: there was no warning. Opioids slow the part of the brain that tells you to breathe, and they also take away the alarm you would normally feel when you cannot get enough air. People describe getting heavier and heavier, a pleasant sinking, nodding off as usual, and then nothing. Many only learned they had overdosed when they woke up to paramedics, or to someone shouting at them.

Some remember a brief moment of feeling very far away, a ringing in the ears, or vision narrowing. With fentanyl, many say they lost consciousness before they could put the drug down. Because there is no felt danger, you cannot rely on "I'll know when I've had too much."

## What it looks like from the outside

- Will not wake up, even to a loud voice or a hard rub on the breastbone with the knuckles.
- Very slow breathing (fewer than about one breath every 5 seconds), shallow breathing, or none at all.
- Gurgling, choking, or deep snoring sounds. This is not sleep; it is often an airway struggling.
- Pinpoint (very small) pupils.
- Blue, gray, or ashen lips and fingernails; on darker skin, gray or ashy color inside the lips and gums.
- Limp body, pale or clammy skin, slow or absent pulse.
- With fentanyl, sometimes stiff chest and jaw muscles ("wooden chest").

## What naloxone (Narcan) does

Naloxone is an opioid antagonist. It pushes opioids off the brain's receptors and restarts breathing, usually within 2 to 5 minutes. It is harmless to someone who has not taken opioids, so if in doubt, give it. It comes as a nasal spray (Narcan, RiVive, and generics, sold over the counter in the US since 2023) and as injections. Nalmefene (Opvee, Zurnai) is a longer-lasting relative.

**What waking up feels like:** for someone with opioid dependence, naloxone causes instant withdrawal. Survivors describe snapping awake confused, sick, sweating, shaking, vomiting, with aches, chills, diarrhea, and intense craving and agitation. Some are angry or frightened and do not remember what happened. This is unpleasant but survivable; the overdose was not.

**The overdose can come back.** Naloxone lasts roughly 30 to 90 minutes. Many opioids, especially methadone, extended-release pills, and large amounts of fentanyl, last longer. When naloxone wears off, the person can stop breathing again. Using more opioids to ease the withdrawal is especially dangerous.

## What a bystander should do, step by step

1. **Check for response.** Shout their name, and rub your knuckles hard on the center of their chest.
2. **Call 911.** Say "Someone is not breathing" or "not responding." Give the address. The dispatcher can coach you.
3. **Give naloxone.** For the nasal spray: lay them on their back, put the nozzle in one nostril, and press the plunger. Follow the instructions on the package.
4. **Support breathing.** If you are trained, give rescue breaths: tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose, and give one breath every 5 seconds. If there is no pulse, start CPR. The 911 dispatcher can guide you.
5. **Give more naloxone after 2 to 3 minutes** if there is no response, using a new device.
6. **Recovery position.** Once they are breathing, roll them onto their side, with the top knee bent and the head tilted slightly back, so they do not choke on vomit.
7. **Stay with them** until help arrives, and for at least a few hours afterward if possible. Do not let them use again right away. Tell paramedics what you know.

**Do not:** put them in a cold bath, inject them with anything else, try to make them vomit, or leave them to "sleep it off."

## Good Samaritan laws

Almost every US state has a Good Samaritan law that gives some legal protection from drug possession charges to people who call 911 for an overdose. Protections differ by state. Fear of police is a major reason people don't call; check your state's law ahead of time.

## Where to get free naloxone

- Pharmacies (over the counter, and many states have standing orders; insurance often covers it).
- Local health departments and syringe service programs, which often give it free.
- NEXT Distro (nextdistro.org) and other mail-based programs.
- Many libraries, schools, and public buildings now keep it on the wall like a fire extinguisher.

## Never use alone

The Never Use Alone hotline (**1-877-696-1996**, US) stays on the phone and sends help if a caller stops responding. Fentanyl test strips can show whether fentanyl is present.

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7). FindTreatment.gov lists treatment programs, including methadone and buprenorphine providers.
