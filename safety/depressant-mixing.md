# Mixing depressants: why combinations kill and what to do

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice. Nothing here gives amounts or instructions for combining drugs. The safest choice is not to combine depressants at all.

## What counts as a depressant

Depressants slow the brain and the body. They include alcohol, opioids (including fentanyl, kratom and tianeptine), benzodiazepines and designer benzodiazepines, barbiturates, Z-drugs such as zolpidem, GHB and GBL, gabapentin and pregabalin, phenibut, muscle relaxants such as carisoprodol, baclofen and tizanidine, sedating antihistamines such as promethazine and diphenhydramine, xylazine, and some antipsychotics and antidepressants.

## Why combinations are so dangerous

Each depressant slows breathing in a slightly different way. Opioids blunt the brainstem's response to rising carbon dioxide. Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol and Z-drugs strengthen the brain's main calming chemical, GABA. Gabapentinoids reduce excitatory signals. When they are combined, these effects do not just add up; they multiply. A combination of amounts that would each be manageable alone can stop someone breathing.

Most deaths blamed on benzodiazepines, gabapentin, or muscle relaxants involve an opioid or alcohol too. The FDA added its strongest warnings to opioid and benzodiazepine labels in 2016, and to gabapentinoids in 2019, because of this.

Combinations also make blackouts and memory loss far more likely, so people may take more without remembering, and vomiting while unconscious can block the airway.

## What it feels like from the inside

People who survived a depressant-mix overdose often describe no warning at all: feeling pleasantly heavy or sleepy, then waking up in an ambulance or hospital, if they woke up. Some remember feeling unable to keep their eyes open, trouble speaking, or a sense of sinking. Because many depressants erase memory, people often cannot say what happened.

## What it looks like from the outside

- Cannot be woken, or wakes only briefly and drifts off again.
- Slow, shallow, irregular or stopped breathing; snoring or gurgling sounds.
- Blue or gray lips and fingertips; cold, clammy skin.
- Limp body; may vomit while unconscious.
- With GHB, sudden collapse; with stimulants mixed in, confusing signs such as agitation and then collapse.

## What to do

1. Try to wake them: shout their name and rub the breastbone firmly with your knuckles.
2. Call 911. Good Samaritan laws in most US states protect people who call for help in an overdose.
3. Give naloxone (Narcan) if there is any chance an opioid is involved, including counterfeit pills. It will not reverse alcohol, benzodiazepines, GHB, or other non-opioids, but it is safe to give and can reverse the opioid part. See [naloxone](../narcan/naloxone.md).
4. If they are not breathing, give rescue breaths or CPR if you know how; the dispatcher can guide you.
5. If they are breathing, place them in the recovery position on their side so they will not choke on vomit.
6. Stay with them until help arrives. Effects can return or deepen.

## Withdrawal is also dangerous

Alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, GHB, carisoprodol, baclofen, phenibut and gabapentinoids can all cause dangerous withdrawal after daily use, including seizures. Stopping should be planned with a medical professional.

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7). Poison Control: **1-800-222-1222**. In an emergency, call **911**.
