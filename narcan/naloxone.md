# Naloxone (Narcan, Kloxxado, RiVive, Zimhi, generic)

> Naloxone saves lives. If someone may be overdosing on an opioid, **call 911 and give naloxone.**

## What it is

Naloxone is an opioid antagonist: a medicine that blocks opioid receptors and knocks opioids such as heroin, fentanyl, oxycodone, and methadone off them. It has no effect on someone who has not taken opioids, cannot get anyone high, and is not addictive.

**Brands:** Narcan nasal spray (over the counter in the US since 2023), RiVive (over the counter), Kloxxado (a stronger nasal spray), Zimhi (prefilled injection), generic nasal spray, and generic injectable vials. Evzio, an auto-injector that talked users through the steps, was discontinued in 2020.

## History

Naloxone was patented in 1961 by Mozes Lewenstein, Jack Fishman, and the Japanese company Sankyo, and approved by the FDA in 1971 as an injection for hospitals. In 1996, harm-reduction workers in Chicago began giving it to people who use drugs. Massachusetts and other states started community programs in the 2000s. The FDA approved Narcan nasal spray in 2015, and in March 2023 approved it for over-the-counter sale.

## How it works

Naloxone binds to opioid receptors more strongly than most opioids, pushing them off and blocking them. This restores breathing, usually within 2 to 5 minutes. It lasts about 30 to 90 minutes.

## What reversal feels like for the person

For someone with opioid dependence, naloxone causes **sudden withdrawal**. Survivors describe snapping awake, confused and panicked, with no memory of passing out. Within minutes come sweating, chills, goosebumps, shaking, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, body aches, a racing heart, and strong craving. Some people are angry or combative because they feel suddenly sick and don't understand what happened. People who don't use opioids regularly may simply wake up groggy.

## What bystanders should expect

- It may take 2 to 3 minutes to work. Keep supporting breathing in the meantime.
- The person may wake up sick, confused, or upset. Speak calmly, explain they overdosed, and tell them help is coming.
- They may vomit. Turn them on their side.
- If they do not respond in 2 to 3 minutes, give a second dose.

## Why a second dose may be needed

- Strong opioids such as fentanyl, carfentanil, and nitazenes may need more than one dose.
- Naloxone often wears off before the opioid does, especially with methadone, extended-release pills, or large fentanyl exposures, so the overdose can return. Stay with the person and get medical care, even if they wake up.
- Using more opioids after reversal to relieve withdrawal is very dangerous.

## Storage and expiry

Keep it at room temperature, away from extreme heat or freezing, and out of direct sunlight. Narcan nasal spray's shelf life was extended to 4 years. Expired naloxone is still better than nothing in an emergency. Keep it where others can find it, and tell friends and family where it is.

## Where to get it, often free

- Any US pharmacy, without a prescription. Insurance and Medicaid often cover it.
- Health departments, syringe service programs, and harm-reduction groups, often free.
- Mail-based programs such as NEXT Distro (nextdistro.org).
- Public naloxone boxes in libraries, schools, bars, and transit stations.

## The law

All 50 US states have laws expanding naloxone access, and most protect people who give it in good faith from civil liability. Most states also have Good Samaritan laws that limit drug charges for people who call 911 for an overdose. Details vary by state.
