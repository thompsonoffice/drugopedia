# Dantrolene (Dantrium)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Direct-acting muscle relaxant that works inside the muscle fiber.

**Brand and street names:** Dantrium capsules, Dantrium IV, Ryanodex.

## History and origins

Approved in 1974; it became the life-saving treatment for malignant hyperthermia, a deadly reaction to anesthetic gases.

## Medical use, then and now

Spasticity, malignant hyperthermia, and neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

## How it entered recreational use

No real misuse: it causes weakness, not pleasure.

## Legal status

Prescription-only.

## What the pills feel like

Dantrium capsules have an orange and tan shell.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Dantrolene feels like weakness rather than sedation: arms and legs feel heavy and tired, sometimes with drowsiness and dizziness.

Long-term oral use can cause serious liver injury, which carries a boxed warning.

It is mostly important as an emergency hospital drug.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (1-2 h)

Dantrolene works directly inside muscle fibers, blocking the release of calcium that makes muscles contract, rather than acting on the brain. Its onset is felt over hours as weakness more than relaxation: the arms and legs feel tired and heavy, grip strength softens, and climbing stairs takes more effort than usual.

Mentally it brings some drowsiness, dizziness and lightheadedness, but no high or calm in the usual sense. Some feel a vague malaise, as if coming down with a cold, and a general reluctance to move. It is a slow, physical change rather than a mental one.

Diarrhea, nausea, loss of appetite and a washed-out feeling are common early effects, and some people develop sensitivity to sunlight.

Medically, the onset reflects absorption from the gut and entry into the brain. Dantrolene blocks calcium release inside muscle cells (ryanodine receptors) and has a half-life of about 9 hours. Muscle relaxants work mostly through central sedation rather than acting on the muscles themselves (dantrolene is the exception), so drowsiness usually arrives together with the loosening of tension.

Clinicians warn that reaction time slows early, often before the person notices, and that dizziness on standing is common, especially in older adults.

### Come-up (2-4 h)

Gradual weakness.

### Peak (4-6 h)

At the peak muscles can feel weak and floppy, and tasks like holding a heavy cup, rising from a chair, or walking any distance become harder. People with spasticity from spinal cord injury, stroke or cerebral palsy may find stiffness much reduced and movement smoother.

Emotionally the peak is tired, sometimes low or irritable, without euphoria. Some report confusion, trouble concentrating or headache. The contrast between a clear mind and weak muscles can feel strange and frustrating.

Long-term oral use can cause serious and sometimes fatal liver damage, so liver tests are monitored. In hospitals, the IV form is a life-saving treatment for malignant hyperthermia, a dangerous reaction to certain anesthetics.

Medically, the peak is when sedation and dizziness are strongest. Several muscle relaxants have anticholinergic or blood-pressure effects as well, which can cause dry mouth, blurred vision, a fast or slow heartbeat, or fainting.

Alone they rarely stop breathing in healthy adults, but combined with opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, or gabapentinoids they add to respiratory depression; carisoprodol in particular was a common part of the deadly 'holy trinity' with opioids and benzodiazepines.

### Plateau (many hours)

The dantrolene plateau is lasting weakness and fatigue, since the drug stays in the body a long time. Limbs feel heavy and tire quickly, and people may need to rest more often or use support when walking. Diarrhea or stomach upset can continue.

Thinking may feel a little slow, and drowsiness can affect driving and work. Mood is generally flat or tired rather than changed in any dramatic way. People often learn to plan activities around the hours when weakness is strongest, sitting to dress or carrying lighter loads, and some find the trade-off between less spasm and less strength takes weeks to balance.

Falls are a real risk, especially when standing up quickly or using stairs, because the muscles that catch a stumble respond more weakly.

### Comedown (about 9 h half-life)

As levels slowly drop, strength gradually returns over a day or more, and spasticity may come back. Some people notice their muscles feel stiff again before they feel energetic again.

Mood is usually unaffected, though fatigue and a washed-out feeling can linger, particularly in the first weeks of treatment. Because the drug clears slowly, missing a dose does not cause a sudden change; strength creeps back gradually, and so does stiffness, giving people time to notice the difference. Doctors usually stop it if no clear benefit appears within several weeks.

Dantrolene is not used recreationally and does not cause cravings or withdrawal, but anyone taking it long-term should watch for yellowing skin, dark urine or abdominal pain.

As levels fall, sedation lifts and muscle tension may return. Short-acting drugs wear off within hours, while drugs with active metabolites (carisoprodol to meprobamate) linger.

People who use carisoprodol, meprobamate, or baclofen daily can develop dependence; stopping suddenly can cause anxiety, insomnia, hallucinations, and seizures, and abrupt baclofen withdrawal can be life-threatening.

### After-effects (next day)

Weakness, fatigue.

### Next day (tired)

Tiredness.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, muscle relaxants mainly cause drowsiness and eased tension. At higher doses, dizziness, heavy sedation, poor coordination, blurred vision, and confusion grow, and some (carisoprodol, meprobamate, orphenadrine, baclofen, tizanidine) bring additional dangers: seizures, heart rhythm problems, very low blood pressure, or coma. With opioids, alcohol, or benzodiazepines, breathing can slow and stop.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

## Overdose note for Dantrolene (Dantrium)

Overdose causes profound weakness and breathing difficulty; liver toxicity with long use.
