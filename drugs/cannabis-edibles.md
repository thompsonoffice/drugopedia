# Cannabis edibles

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Foods, candies and drinks containing THC.

**Brand and street names:** Gummies, chocolates, brownies, cookies, beverages, tinctures.

## History and origins

Edible cannabis, such as bhang in India, is ancient. Modern commercial edibles grew with legalization; Colorado added labeling and serving rules after early emergency visits in 2014.

## Medical use, then and now

Oral THC is the route of dronabinol and many medical-market products for sleep, pain and nausea.

## How it entered recreational use

Edibles are popular because there is no smoke, but they cause the most accidental overdoses, especially in children and new users.

## Legal status

Same as cannabis; legal markets require child-resistant packaging and labels, but illicit edibles often copy candy brands.

## What the products look and feel like

Edibles look exactly like normal candy or baked goods, which is why they must be locked away from children and pets. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Edibles feel different from smoked cannabis: slow to start, heavier and more body-centered, with a stronger 'stoned', dreamy feeling because the liver makes 11-hydroxy-THC.

The delay is the main danger: people think nothing is happening and take more, then everything arrives at once.

An edible high can last most of a day, with confusion, vomiting, panic, or hallucinations at high levels. Children who eat edibles can become very drowsy or have seizures.

Tolerance to the high builds quickly with daily use and fades after a break of a few weeks; people returning after a break, or switching to concentrates or edibles, are often surprised by how strong the effect is.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (30 min-2 h)

Effects usually begin 30 minutes to 2 hours after eating, slower on a full stomach.

Medically, the onset depends on the route. Swallowed THC is converted by the liver to 11-hydroxy-THC, which is more potent. Inhaled THC reaches the brain within minutes; swallowed THC is absorbed slowly and turned by the liver into 11-hydroxy-THC, which is more potent and longer-lasting, which is why edibles feel stronger and start so much later.

Early body signs include a faster heartbeat, red eyes, dry mouth and, in some people, a drop in blood pressure on standing that can cause fainting.

### Come-up (1-3 h)

The rise is gradual: body heaviness, warmth and giddiness.

### Peak (2-4 h)

The peak, around 2 to 4 hours, is the strongest and longest-feeling high, sometimes with time loss and confusion.

Medically, the peak is when CB1 activation is greatest. Short-term memory, attention, coordination and time perception are most affected, and anxiety or paranoia can appear, especially with high-THC products or in people prone to psychosis.

Cannabis alone does not stop breathing, so fatal overdose is not expected, but emergency visits for panic, vomiting, fast heart rate, and psychosis are common, and children who eat edibles can become very sedated.

### Plateau (4-6 h)

The plateau can last for hours.

### Comedown (6-12 h)

It fades slowly over 6 to 8 hours or more.

As levels fall, the effect fades gradually because THC slowly leaves fat stores. Many people feel tired, hungry or foggy.

Daily users can develop cannabis use disorder and, with heavy long-term use, cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (repeated severe vomiting relieved by hot showers). Withdrawal causes irritability, poor sleep and low appetite for one to two weeks.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Sleepiness and lingering fog.

### Next day

Hangover-like grogginess often continues into the next day.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses cannabis brings relaxation, mild euphoria, giggliness, and heightened senses. At higher doses, time distortion, anxiety, paranoia, a racing heart, nausea, dizziness and, for some, hallucinations or panic appear. Very high doses, especially edibles, can cause hours of confusion, vomiting and frightening psychotic symptoms.

Taking more before the first has peaked is the classic edible mistake; effects can then become overwhelming for many hours.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Cannabis edibles

Edibles are a leading cause of cannabis emergency visits and child poisonings; call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) if a child eats one.

Read the shared page: [Cannabis safety: edibles, high-potency products, synthetic cannabinoids](../safety/cannabis-safety.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
