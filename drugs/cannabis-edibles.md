# Cannabis edibles

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Foods, candies and drinks containing THC.

**Brand and street names:** Gummies, chocolates, brownies, cookies, beverages, tinctures.

## History and origins

Edible cannabis, such as bhang in India, is ancient. Modern commercial edibles grew with legalization; Colorado added labeling and serving rules after early emergency visits in 2014.

## Medical use, then and now

Oral THC is the route of dronabinol and many medical-market products for sleep, pain and nausea.

## How it entered recreational use

Edibles are popular because there is no smoke, but they cause the most accidental overdoses, especially in children and new users.

## Legal status

Same as cannabis; legal markets require child-resistant packaging and labels, but illicit edibles often copy candy brands.

## What the products look and feel like

Edibles look exactly like normal candy or baked goods, which is why they must be locked away from children and pets. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Edibles feel different from smoked cannabis: slow to start, heavier and more body-centered, with a stronger 'stoned', dreamy feeling because the liver makes 11-hydroxy-THC.

The delay is the main danger: people think nothing is happening and take more, then everything arrives at once.

An edible high can last most of a day, with confusion, vomiting, panic, or hallucinations at high levels. Children who eat edibles can become very drowsy or have seizures.

Tolerance to the high builds quickly with daily use and fades after a break of a few weeks; people returning after a break, or switching to concentrates or edibles, are often surprised by how strong the effect is.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (30 min-2 h)

Edibles (gummies, chocolates, baked goods, drinks) deliver THC through the gut, so the onset is slow, often taking from half an hour to two hours or more. The first signs are subtle: a warm heaviness in the limbs, a slight tingle, dry mouth and then a growing pulse in the chest. The liver converts THC into a stronger, longer-lasting form, so effects can build much higher than expected.

Mentally the onset often begins with uncertainty and then a gradual or sudden arrival of euphoria, giggles, distorted time and a dreamy, floating mind. Many people mistakenly think the edible isn't working and take more, only for both portions to hit together.

This delayed, stacking effect is the most common reason for edible emergencies, including severe anxiety, vomiting, fainting and racing heart. Edibles that look like candy are a serious poisoning risk for children and pets.

Swallowed THC is absorbed slowly and unevenly from the gut, and only a fraction survives first-pass liver metabolism; much is converted to 11-hydroxy-THC, which crosses into the brain readily.

Food in the stomach can delay onset by an hour or more, which is the main reason people take extra before the first amount works.

### Come-up (1-3 h)

The rise is gradual: body heaviness, warmth and giddiness.

### Peak (2-4 h)

At the peak, a few hours in, the body may feel very heavy, sedated and sunk into bed or couch, with a racing or pounding heart, dry mouth, red eyes and sometimes nausea, chills or dizziness on standing. Some feel cold and shivery, others hot and sweaty, and the limbs can feel too heavy to lift.

Mentally the peak is often more intense and body-centered than smoking: strong euphoria, deep relaxation, vivid sensations or, for many, a frightening sense of being trapped in a high that won't end, paranoia and confusion about time.

Children who eat edibles may become very drowsy, unsteady or unresponsive and need urgent medical care. Adults with heart disease may be at risk from the raised heart rate. A calm room, sips of water, a snack and a trusted person usually help; poison control can advise.

At the peak, 11-hydroxy-THC levels can be high relative to THC, giving a heavier, more sedating and more disorienting effect than smoked cannabis.

Emergency departments see edible cases with vomiting, fast heart rate, panic and, in children, deep sedation, low breathing effort and seizures.

### Plateau (4-6 h)

The plateau is long, often several hours. The body stays heavy and slow, appetite can be strong, and some people fall asleep for many hours. The mouth stays dry, the eyes heavy, and getting up may bring dizziness. Waves of the high may roll in and out, and some people feel sick and need to lie still.

Mentally thinking is slowed and memory poor; some feel peaceful, others anxious that the effect will never stop. Reassurance that it will fade helps. Some find music, films or food deeply absorbing; others feel stuck in loops of worry, checking their pulse or watching the clock.

Driving or using machinery is unsafe for the full duration, which can stretch most of the day. Mixing with alcohol can worsen nausea and dizziness and may lead to vomiting.

### Comedown (6-12 h)

The comedown can last into the next day, with grogginess, dry mouth and a heavy, foggy head sometimes called an 'edible hangover.' Some wake with a stiff body, sticky mouth and a lingering sense of being slowed, which can last until midday. Appetite may stay high.

Mentally there may be slowness, low motivation and mild anxiety or embarrassment after an overwhelming experience. Some feel detached or not quite themselves for a while, which usually resolves with sleep, food and time.

Storing edibles locked away from children and pets, and waiting fully before taking more, prevent most emergencies. For adults, slow and patient experience with edibles is the main safeguard; panic during an overdose usually passes, but chest pain or fainting needs care.

Effects persist for many hours as the gut keeps absorbing THC and the liver keeps producing 11-hydroxy-THC.

Next-day impairment is common; children may need hospital observation until fully awake.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Sleepiness and lingering fog.

### Next day

Hangover-like grogginess often continues into the next day.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses cannabis brings relaxation, mild euphoria, giggliness, and heightened senses. At higher doses, time distortion, anxiety, paranoia, a racing heart, nausea, dizziness and, for some, hallucinations or panic appear. Very high doses, especially edibles, can cause hours of confusion, vomiting and frightening psychotic symptoms.

Taking more before the first has peaked is the classic edible mistake; effects can then become overwhelming for many hours.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Cannabis edibles

Edibles are a leading cause of cannabis emergency visits and child poisonings; call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) if a child eats one.
