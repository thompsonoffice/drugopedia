# Aerosols and propellants (dusters, spray cans)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Pressurized products whose propellant gases are inhaled.

**Brand and street names:** Computer dusters (difluoroethane), deodorant and hair sprays, cooking sprays, whipped-cream cans.

## History and origins

Duster misuse rose in the 2000s and 2010s; difluoroethane is a frequent cause of inhalant deaths and of car crashes.

## Medical use, then and now

None.

## How it entered recreational use

Used because products are cheap, legal and in every home.

## Legal status

Legal products; misuse is illegal in some states.

## What the products look and feel like

Ordinary spray cans; dusters often contain a bitter additive that does not prevent misuse.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Dusters cause a brief, intense dizziness and ringing in the ears, often followed by blackout.

The propellant makes the heart sensitive to adrenaline, so sudden death can occur; the very cold gas can freeze the throat.

People found unconscious holding a can are a common emergency, and drivers have crashed after using them.

Tolerance can develop with regular use, but the danger of sudden death does not decrease.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (seconds)

Aerosols (computer dusters, spray cans) hit instantly: a cold, numb rush and buzzing ears.

Mentally a brief drunken euphoria.

Frostbite of the mouth and throat can occur.

Propellants such as difluoroethane enter the brain within seconds.

Ear ringing and dizziness appear almost immediately.

### Come-up (seconds)

A rush of dizziness.

### Peak (under 1 min)

The peak is seconds long.

Emotionally dreamy.

Inhalants can stop the heart suddenly ('sudden sniffing death'), even on a first use, and can cause suffocation, choking on vomit, and permanent brain, nerve, liver and kidney damage.

At peak, fluorocarbons sensitize the heart to adrenaline, causing sudden fatal arrhythmias.

The rapid expansion of gas can freeze the airway, causing swelling and choking.

### Plateau (1-2 min)

Effects fade quickly.

Re-use common.

Dizziness.

### Comedown (2-10 min)

The comedown is headache.

Mood flat.

Brain damage risk.

Effects end within minutes.

Repeated use damages the heart, liver and kidneys.

### After-effects (hours)

Headache and confusion.

### Next day (none)

Usually none, but repeated use harms organs.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower exposures inhalants cause brief dizziness, giggling and lightheadedness. At higher exposures, confusion, hallucinations, loss of coordination and unconsciousness occur, and the risk of suffocation and sudden heart rhythm death rises sharply.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

Sudden heart rhythm death, suffocation, frostbite of the airway, and crashes.
