# Delta-8-THC, THC-O and HHC (hemp-derived cannabinoids)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic or converted cannabinoids marketed from hemp.

**Brand and street names:** Delta-8, D8, THC-O acetate, HHC, THCP; sold as gummies, vapes and 'legal weed'.

## History and origins

After the 2018 US Farm Bill legalized hemp with low delta-9-THC, companies began chemically converting hemp CBD into delta-8-THC, and later HHC and THC-O, selling them in gas stations and online.

## Medical use, then and now

None are approved medicines.

## How it entered recreational use

Marketed as a legal or 'lighter' high; FDA and poison centers report rising exposures, many in children.

## Legal status

A legal gray area. The DEA stated in 2023 that THC-O is a controlled substance; many states ban delta-8 and HHC. A 2025 federal law redefined hemp to close this loophole, effective in late 2026.

## What the products look and feel like

Often sold as brightly colored gummies or disposable vape pens. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Delta-8 feels like a milder, clearer version of THC; HHC sits between delta-8 and delta-9; THC-O is described as slow and heavy. All act on the same CB1 receptor.

The main concern is manufacturing: products are made by chemical reactions in unregulated labs and may contain leftover acids, solvents, metals or unknown by-products.

Labels are often wrong, so a product sold as delta-8 may contain delta-9-THC or synthetic cannabinoids.

Tolerance to the high builds quickly with daily use and fades after a break of a few weeks; people returning after a break, or switching to concentrates or edibles, are often surprised by how strong the effect is.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (minutes)

Inhaled effects begin within minutes; edibles take 30 minutes to 2 hours.

Medically, the onset depends on the route. These compounds act at CB1 like THC. Inhaled THC reaches the brain within minutes; swallowed THC is absorbed slowly and turned by the liver into 11-hydroxy-THC, which is more potent and longer-lasting, which is why edibles feel stronger and start so much later.

Early body signs include a faster heartbeat, red eyes, dry mouth and, in some people, a drop in blood pressure on standing that can cause fainting.

### Come-up (5-15 min)

A gradual relaxation and lightness.

### Peak (15-45 min)

The peak brings typical cannabis effects: time distortion, appetite and sleepiness, with possible anxiety.

Medically, the peak is when CB1 activation is greatest. Short-term memory, attention, coordination and time perception are most affected, and anxiety or paranoia can appear, especially with high-THC products or in people prone to psychosis.

Cannabis alone does not stop breathing, so fatal overdose is not expected, but emergency visits for panic, vomiting, fast heart rate, and psychosis are common, and children who eat edibles can become very sedated.

### Plateau (1 h)

Plateau of an hour (vapes) to several hours (edibles).

### Comedown (2-3 h)

Gradual fade.

As levels fall, the effect fades gradually because THC slowly leaves fat stores. Many people feel tired, hungry or foggy.

Daily users can develop cannabis use disorder and, with heavy long-term use, cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (repeated severe vomiting relieved by hot showers). Withdrawal causes irritability, poor sleep and low appetite for one to two weeks.

### After-effects (3-5 h)

Dry mouth and grogginess.

### Next day (next morning)

Possible next-day fog, particularly with edibles.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses cannabis brings relaxation, mild euphoria, giggliness, and heightened senses. At higher doses, time distortion, anxiety, paranoia, a racing heart, nausea, dizziness and, for some, hallucinations or panic appear. Very high doses, especially edibles, can cause hours of confusion, vomiting and frightening psychotic symptoms.

Strength varies from batch to batch, so the jump from mild to overwhelming is unpredictable.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Delta-8-THC, THC-O and HHC (hemp-derived cannabinoids)

FDA and poison centers report vomiting, hallucinations, confusion and loss of consciousness, often in children.
