# Lorazepam (Ativan)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Never stop a daily benzodiazepine suddenly: withdrawal can cause seizures. Counterfeit pills often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines.

## At a glance

Intermediate-acting benzodiazepine processed without CYP enzymes.

**Brand and street names:** Ativan, Loreev XR, Temesta (Europe).

## History and origins

Introduced by Wyeth in 1977.

## Medical use, then and now

Anxiety, status epilepticus (IV), alcohol withdrawal, sedation before procedures, and nausea from chemotherapy.

## How it entered recreational use

Misused for calm and sleep.

## Legal status

US Schedule IV. UK Class C.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Lorazepam is mostly immediate-release; Loreev XR is a once-daily extended-release capsule approved in 2021 for people already stable on lorazepam, giving steadier levels.

## What the pills feel like

Per the DailyMed Ativan label, the tablets are small, white, and five-sided (one strength shield-shaped, another a regular pentagon), with a raised 'A' on one side and a score line on the other; generics are small and round or five-sided, often scored.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Ativan is described as a heavy, sleepy, very calming effect, more sedating than Xanax and strongly amnesic. People in hospitals often remember little of procedures done under it.

Its euphoria is mild; the main feeling is safety and sleepiness.

Tolerance to the calming and sleep effects of benzodiazepines builds within weeks, while memory loss and the danger of mixing with alcohol or opioids remain. People taking them daily often feel little except relief from anxiety, and stopping suddenly can cause seizures.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (20-40 min)

Lorazepam (Ativan) is a medium-acting, fairly strong benzodiazepine. Swallowed or dissolved under the tongue, it settles in over a short while: the shoulders drop, the jaw unclenches, a heaviness gathers behind the eyes and the chest loosens as if a tight band has been let out.

Mentally the onset feels like someone turning down the volume on fear. Racing thoughts slow, the 'what if' loop goes quiet, and many people describe a soft, blanketed feeling of safety. It is more sedating and less floaty than alprazolam, and often comes with a pull toward sleep.

Light-headedness and unsteadiness on standing appear early, and reaction time is already slowed even when the person feels only relaxed.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug is absorbed and enters the brain. Lorazepam is glucuronidated in the liver without CYP enzymes, so it is less affected by liver disease and drug interactions; half-life 10 to 20 hours. As it binds to GABA-A receptors, brain activity slows: anxiety eases, muscles relax, and reaction time lengthens.

Clinicians notice early signs such as slower speech, drooping eyelids, and reduced coordination. People often underestimate how impaired they already are at this stage, which is why driving is dangerous.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

Sleepiness and calm build.

### Peak (1.5-2.5 h)

At its peak the body feels heavy, warm and loose; limbs are slow to respond, speech may slur slightly, and coordination is clumsy. Many people simply fall asleep.

Mentally the peak is calm, indifferent and foggy. Worries feel distant and unimportant, but so do details: lorazepam is strongly linked to anterograde amnesia, so people often cannot remember conversations or actions from this window, sometimes while appearing normal to others.

With alcohol, opioids or other sedatives the peak can slide into dangerously slowed breathing and unresponsiveness.

Medically, the peak is when sedation, muscle relaxation, and memory impairment are strongest. Benzodiazepines can block the formation of new memories (anterograde amnesia), so people may act normally yet remember nothing later.

On their own, benzodiazepines rarely stop breathing in healthy adults, but combined with opioids or alcohol they greatly deepen respiratory depression. The FDA added its strongest boxed warning about this combination in 2016.

### Plateau (2-8 h)

The plateau lasts several hours of steady, quiet sedation. The body remains slack and drowsy, and balance stays poor, which raises the risk of falls, especially in older people.

Mentally the state is muted and even: little anxiety, little drive and weak short-term memory. Some feel a gentle contentment; others feel blank or dull.

Because it lasts long enough to overlap, repeated doses during the plateau stack up and deepen sedation.

### Comedown (8-12 h total)

The comedown is gradual: heaviness thins into tiredness, a foggy head and sometimes a mild headache.

Emotionally anxiety may creep back, sometimes stronger than before ('rebound anxiety'), along with irritability or a flat mood.

Daily use builds tolerance and dependence within weeks. Stopping suddenly can cause severe anxiety, insomnia, tremor, hallucinations and seizures, so tapering with a prescriber is essential.

As levels fall, the calming effect wears off. With short-acting benzodiazepines, rebound anxiety and insomnia can be sharp. Long-acting ones and their active metabolites can linger for days, causing next-day drowsiness and impaired driving.

In people who take them daily, falling levels can trigger withdrawal, which in severe cases includes seizures and delirium. This is why stopping should be a slow medical taper, not a sudden stop.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Grogginess and memory gaps.

### Next day

Daily use leads to dependence and potentially dangerous withdrawal.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, benzodiazepines mainly ease anxiety and muscle tension, with mild calm and slight drowsiness. At higher doses, sedation, clumsiness, slurred speech, disinhibition, and memory loss grow, and blackouts become likely. As effects keep rising, people become very difficult to wake, confused, and unable to stand, and when opioids or alcohol are involved, breathing slows and can stop: these are overdose warning signs.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance builds quickly, liver function and age change how fast benzodiazepines are cleared (older adults clear many much more slowly), and many medicines block the CYP3A4 enzyme that breaks down alprazolam, midazolam, and triazolam. Alcohol and opioids multiply the sedation and breathing risk. Stronger effects shade into blackouts, falls, and, in combinations, stopped breathing.

## Overdose note for Lorazepam (Ativan)

Strong amnesia and sedation; dangerous with opioids and alcohol. IV use requires breathing monitoring.
