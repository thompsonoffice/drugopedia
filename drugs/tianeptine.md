# Tianeptine ('gas station heroin')

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Atypical antidepressant (tricyclic structure) that is also a full mu-opioid agonist, discovered in 2014.

**Brand and street names:** Prescription: Stablon, Coaxil, Tatinol (Europe, Asia, Latin America). US products: Zaza, Tianaa, Pegasus, Neptune's Fix, sold in gas stations and smoke shops as 'supplements'.

## History and origins

Developed by the French company Servier in the 1960s and marketed as an antidepressant in 1989. In 2014, researchers at Columbia showed it activates mu-opioid receptors (Gassaway et al.). It was never approved in the US, but from the 2010s it was sold in convenience stores. The FDA warned against it in 2018, 2022, and again in 2025 after clusters of seizures, comas, and deaths, some linked to Neptune's Fix products that also contained synthetic cannabinoids. Several US states have banned it.

## Medical use, then and now

Prescribed for depression and anxiety in some countries. Not approved in the US.

## How it entered recreational use

Sold as a 'nootropic' or mood supplement, it became known as 'gas station heroin' because people develop opioid-like dependence and sometimes use it many times a day.

## Legal status

Not federally controlled in the US, but banned in a growing number of states (including Michigan, Alabama, Florida, Ohio, and others). Prescription-only elsewhere; banned in the UK under the Psychoactive Substances Act.

## What the pills feel like

Forms vary by country and manufacturer; see the brand list above. Ask a pharmacist to describe the product you have.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Tianeptine's effect is described as a short, clean, mildly stimulating opioid-like euphoria: mood lift, motivation, and calm, with a light body warmth. At higher amounts it becomes more clearly opioid-like, with itch, nausea, and sedation.

Because it lasts only 2 to 3 hours, many people take it repeatedly through the day. Users describe the withdrawal as disproportionately harsh compared with how mild the high felt.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (30-60 min)

Tianeptine is an antidepressant sold by prescription in parts of Europe and Asia, and in the US it has been sold at gas stations under names like 'Tianaa' and 'ZaZa' despite FDA warnings. At much more than medical amounts it acts as a full mu-opioid agonist. Swallowed, it comes on fast: a warm, buzzing lift in the chest, a loosening of the body and a sudden easing of anxiety.

Mentally the onset is often described as bright and stimulating more than sleepy: a burst of contentment, sociability and confidence, sometimes compared to a short hydrocodone high with an antidepressant glow.

It is very short-acting, so the onset is already the start of the decline, which is why people who misuse it often take it many times a day.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Tianeptine has a short half-life of about 2.5 hours and is broken down mostly by beta-oxidation, not CYP enzymes. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (15-30 min)

The come-up is quick, 15 to 30 minutes: calm, a bit of energy, and a warm glow.

### Peak (60-90 min)

At its peak the body feels warm and light, with mild itching, sweating, narrowed pupils and sometimes nausea. At higher amounts heaviness and nodding take over.

Mentally the peak is a clean-feeling euphoria and calm; worries vanish and people feel chatty and upbeat. As amounts rise, it becomes dreamier and more sedated.

Overdose with slowed breathing, confusion, agitation and heart problems has been reported, often with other drugs. Naloxone reverses the opioid part.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (under 1 h)

The plateau is short, often under two hours. The warmth thins into a mild relaxed buzz while sweating and dry mouth continue.

Mentally the brightness fades quickly into restlessness and a desire for more; people often watch the clock for the next dose.

This rapid cycle drives heavy, compulsive use and quick tolerance.

### Comedown (2-3 h)

The comedown is fast and unpleasant: aches, yawning, goosebumps, sweating and a crawling discomfort in the legs.

Emotionally people describe sharp anxiety, irritability and low mood, sometimes worse than with classic opioids because the antidepressant lift also disappears.

Withdrawal from heavy daily use can be severe, with insomnia, agitation, diarrhea and intense craving, and has sometimes needed hospital care. Medical help with tapering or buprenorphine is recommended.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (3-6 h)

After-effects can include nausea, headache, and restlessness.

### Next day

With daily repeated use, withdrawal sets in quickly: severe anxiety, sweating, diarrhea, agitation, and insomnia, sometimes lasting weeks.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

At lower doses tianeptine acts like an antidepressant with little opioid feeling; at higher doses opioid effects appear (euphoria, itch, nausea, sedation). Very high or repeated doses have caused agitation, confusion, seizures, coma, and slowed breathing.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/tianeptine.md): Tablets (prescription), capsules, powders, and liquid shots sold in stores.

Not covered: Injection of tianeptine powder has been reported and caused serious harm, but is not covered.

## Overdose note for Tianeptine ('gas station heroin')

Seizures, comas, and dangerous heart effects have been reported, especially from US store products, some of which were contaminated. Naloxone reverses its opioid effects. Overdose is much more likely when combined with other sedatives.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
