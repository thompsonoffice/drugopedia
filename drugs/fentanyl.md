# Fentanyl (pharmaceutical)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Synthetic opioid of the 4-anilidopiperidine family; very potent full mu-opioid agonist, roughly 50 to 100 times stronger than morphine by weight.

**Brand and street names:** Sublimaze (injection), Duragesic (patch), Actiq (lozenge on a stick), Fentora (buccal tablet), Abstral (sublingual tablet), Subsys (sublingual spray), Lazanda and Instanyl (nasal sprays), Onsolis (buccal film), Ionsys (iontophoretic system, discontinued).

## History and origins

Paul Janssen synthesized fentanyl in Belgium in 1960. It entered anesthesia as Sublimaze in the 1960s and the Duragesic patch came in 1990. Fast-acting 'breakthrough' products for cancer pain followed in the 2000s. Insys Therapeutics executives were convicted in 2019 for bribing doctors to prescribe the Subsys spray.

## Medical use, then and now

Anesthesia, intensive care, severe pain in hospitals, chronic cancer pain (patches), and breakthrough cancer pain in opioid-tolerant patients.

## How it entered recreational use

Pharmaceutical fentanyl patches have been diverted and misused since the 1990s. Illicit fentanyl is covered on its own page.

## Legal status

US Schedule II. Most analogs are Schedule I, and since 2018 fentanyl-related substances are controlled as a class.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Fentanyl's long-acting form is the transdermal patch (Duragesic and generics), which releases drug over 72 hours; the fast-acting forms (Actiq, Fentora, Abstral, Subsys, Lazanda) are for breakthrough cancer pain only. Patches hold a large reserve; heat and damage are deadly.

**Extra risks of extended-release:** Extended-release products hold far more drug than a single immediate-release tablet, released slowly over many hours. If the release system is damaged, chewed, crushed, dissolved, or taken with alcohol (for some products), the whole reserve can be released at once, which has caused many overdose deaths. ER products are meant only for people with established opioid tolerance and ongoing pain, and they should never be split or shared. Because they last so long, overdose can develop slowly, often during sleep.

## What the pills feel like

Fentanyl patches are thin, flexible, rectangular or rounded-rectangle adhesive patches sealed in individual pouches; larger patches deliver more drug. Actiq is a solid lozenge on a plastic handle, like a lollipop. Fentora is a flat, round buccal tablet.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Fentanyl's effect is described as fast, intense, and short: a strong wave of warmth and sedation, more 'blank' and sleepy than euphoric, with less of the dreamy, glowing warmth of heroin or morphine. Many people say it hits hard and wears off quickly, leaving them wanting more.

From a patch, the effect is slow and constant: steady pain relief, drowsiness, and nausea rather than euphoria.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (IV seconds)

Fentanyl's onset is extremely fast when smoked, snorted or injected, often within seconds to a minute. People describe a sudden, intense warmth and heaviness rushing through the body, with the limbs going heavy almost at once. As a medical patch it starts very slowly, over many hours, with little sense of onset.

Mentally, the rush is brief and intense: a surge of relief, calm and pleasure, quickly followed by heavy sedation. Many people describe fentanyl as 'less euphoric and more sleepy' than heroin, with a shorter, more abrupt high.

Physical signs include pinpoint pupils, a dry mouth, sometimes itching and a flushed face. A rare but dangerous effect is chest wall rigidity ('wooden chest'), where breathing muscles stiffen and breathing becomes very difficult, sometimes within minutes.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Fentanyl is very fat-soluble, reaching the brain quickly, and is metabolized by CYP3A4; it redistributes into fat, so its effect after one IV dose is short but repeated exposure builds up. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (IV under 1 min)

There is almost no come-up; it arrives all at once, a fast drop into sedation more than a climb into euphoria.

### Peak (IV 3-5 min)

The peak comes quickly and is deeply sedating. The body feels extremely heavy, and people often lose track of their surroundings. Breathing slows and can stop with little warning.

The emotional tone is a short-lived calm that shades rapidly into unconsciousness. Many people describe 'blacking out' rather than enjoying a high. Memory of the experience can be patchy.

This is when overdose is most likely. Fentanyl's strength means tiny differences in amount cause huge differences in effect, and street fentanyl is often combined with xylazine or other sedatives. Signs of overdose include unresponsiveness, gurgling, very slow or stopped breathing and blue lips; naloxone may need to be given more than once.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (5-30 min)

With street fentanyl, the plateau is short, with heavy nodding and drowsiness. The body stays heavy and warm, and itching may continue.

Mentally, people are often very sedated or semi-conscious, with little awareness of time. The pleasant feeling fades quickly, which often leads to repeated use.

Breathing remains suppressed, and people who use alone are at very high risk. With patches, the plateau lasts for days, with steady pain relief and drowsiness.

### Comedown (30-60 min)

Fentanyl's comedown is fast. The heaviness lifts, and people may suddenly feel awake and uncomfortable.

Emotionally, the comedown often brings strong craving and restlessness, more quickly than with heroin.

In people with dependence, withdrawal can begin within a few hours, with sweating, yawning, goosebumps, cramps and anxiety. Repeated use to avoid this cycle is very dangerous.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (1-4 h)

From patches, effects persist for many hours after removal because drug remains in the skin.

### Next day

After repeated use, withdrawal comes quickly and is intense.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/fentanyl.md): IV in anesthesia and intensive care; IM in some settings.
- [Transdermal (skin patches)](../routes/transdermal/fentanyl.md): Duragesic and generic patches.
- [Sublingual and buccal (under the tongue or in the cheek)](../routes/sublingual-buccal/fentanyl.md): Actiq, Fentora, Abstral, Subsys, Onsolis.
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/fentanyl.md): Lazanda/Instanyl sprays; in some hospitals and ambulances for pain.
- [Inhaled and smoked](../routes/inhaled-smoked/fentanyl.md): Nebulized fentanyl is used occasionally in hospitals; smoking is a common illicit route.

Not covered: Swallowed fentanyl is poorly absorbed, so oral is not a medical route. No rectal products.

## Overdose note for Fentanyl (pharmaceutical)

Fentanyl overdose can happen within seconds to minutes. It can cause 'wooden chest' (chest-wall rigidity), where the breathing muscles go stiff. More than one naloxone dose may be needed. Heat on a patch speeds release.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
