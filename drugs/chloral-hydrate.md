# Chloral hydrate

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

The oldest synthetic sleeping drug; turned into trichloroethanol in the body.

**Brand and street names:** Noctec, Somnote; 'knockout drops' or 'Mickey Finn' (historic).

## History and origins

Synthesized in 1832 by Justus von Liebig and introduced as a sleep drug in 1869. 'Mickey Finn' drinks spiked with chloral hydrate were used to rob people in 19th-century Chicago.

## Medical use, then and now

Formerly insomnia and sedation of children for procedures; largely abandoned and no longer FDA-approved.

## How it entered recreational use

Historic misuse and drug-facilitated crime.

## Legal status

US Schedule IV.

## What the pills feel like

Historically capsules and syrup.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Chloral hydrate's character is a fast, heavy sleep with a harsh taste and stomach irritation.

Its safety margin is narrow, and it can cause dangerous heart rhythms.

With alcohol it is especially dangerous (the 'Mickey Finn').

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (about 30 min)

Chloral hydrate, one of the oldest sleep drugs (introduced in 1869), starts with an unpleasant, burning, acrid taste and a warm, irritated feeling in the throat and stomach that can bring nausea or belching. Within about half an hour a quick, heavy drowsiness follows: the head grows heavy, the eyelids droop and the limbs slow.

Mentally it brings a sleepy, dull calm, sometimes with a brief giddiness or lightheaded, slightly drunk feeling before sleep arrives. It was once a favorite of Victorian writers and was used in children for medical scans because it reliably put them to sleep.

Historically, it was slipped into alcohol as 'knockout drops' — the original 'Mickey Finn' — because the combination quickly overpowered drinkers.

Medically, the onset reflects absorption and entry into the brain. Its active metabolite trichloroethanol has a half-life of about 8 to 10 hours. Most hypnotics are designed to work quickly so the person falls asleep soon after taking them.

Clinicians advise taking hypnotics only right before bed, because people who stay awake can experience confusion, hallucinations, and memory loss.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

Heaviness.

### Peak (about 1 h)

At the peak there is strong sedation and clumsiness. Most people fall asleep quickly and deeply, often snoring, with slow, regular breathing and a heavy, motionless body. If awake, they sway, slur and struggle to keep their eyes open.

Emotionally the peak is foggy and drowsy, with little awareness of time and sometimes a muddled, dreamlike confusion. In some people, especially children and older adults, it can paradoxically cause agitation or excitement.

Overdose can cause dangerous heart rhythms, vomiting, stomach bleeding, low blood pressure and slowed breathing, and it is much more dangerous with alcohol, which also slows its breakdown. Its effect can be unpredictable.

Medically, the peak is when sleep pressure is strongest. People who resist sleep at this point may walk, eat, talk, drive, or send messages with no memory afterward ('complex sleep behaviors'), which led to a 2019 FDA boxed warning for zolpidem, zaleplon, and eszopiclone.

Combined with alcohol, opioids, or other sedatives, breathing can slow dangerously, and amnesia is much more likely.

### Plateau (sleep)

The chloral hydrate plateau is usually deep sleep for several hours. The body lies heavy and still, breathing slow and deep, and the person may be hard to rouse. Some people wake briefly with stomach discomfort, a sour taste or the need to vomit. The skin may feel cool and the pulse slow, and breathing should be watched closely if any other sedative or alcohol was involved, because the combination can deepen sleep into coma.

If awake, thinking is very dull and confused, and the person may be disoriented about time and place. Memory for this stretch is patchy.

Stomach upset may continue, and repeated nightly use quickly leads to tolerance, so people needed more to get the same sleep, which raised overdose risk.

### Comedown (4-8 h total)

Waking is groggy, sometimes with nausea, a heavy head, a lingering acrid taste and an irritated stomach. Coordination may be off for the first part of the morning, and some people feel a dull headache. The stomach may feel raw or burning for hours, and people often have little appetite until later in the day; drinking water and eating bland food is common to settle it.

Mood is flat and slow, and some people feel irritable or low. Long-term users described feeling dulled and lethargic during the day.

Dependence and withdrawal can occur after regular use, with tolerance building quickly; withdrawal resembles alcohol withdrawal and can include tremor, delirium and seizures. It is rarely used today.

As levels fall, most people sleep through the comedown. Those who wake early can feel groggy, unsteady, and slow, and next-morning impairment can affect driving, especially in women and older adults.

Nightly use can cause tolerance and rebound insomnia on stopping; some hypnotics (GHB, methaqualone, older drugs) cause severe dependence and dangerous withdrawal.

### After-effects (8-24 h)

Stomach upset.

### Next day (hangover)

Hangover.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, hypnotics bring sleepiness and calm. At higher doses, or when the person stays awake, confusion, memory loss, hallucinations, and sleep-walking or sleep-driving become likely. With alcohol or opioids, or with drugs like GHB and methaqualone, effects can tip into unconsciousness, vomiting while unconscious, and stopped breathing.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

Overdose causes coma, irregular heartbeat, stomach bleeding, and death.
