# Illicit fentanyl

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Illegally manufactured fentanyl and fentanyl analogs (such as carfentanil, acetylfentanyl, and others), sold as powder or pressed into counterfeit pills.

**Brand and street names:** No brands. Street names include "fent", "fetty", "blues", "M30s", "dance fever", "China white", "tango and cash".

## History and origins

Isolated outbreaks of street fentanyl appeared in the US from the late 1970s ('China white'). From around 2013, illegally made fentanyl, often from chemicals produced overseas, replaced much of the heroin supply in North America. By 2016 it was the leading cause of overdose death, and synthetic opioids caused around 70,000 US deaths a year at the peak around 2022 to 2023. Newer adulterants include xylazine ('tranq'), an animal sedative that causes severe wounds, and nitazenes, a separate family of synthetic opioids.

## Medical use, then and now

None. Illicit fentanyl is not made to any medical standard.

## How it entered recreational use

Most people who die from fentanyl did not know they were taking it: it is mixed into heroin, counterfeit oxycodone and Xanax pills, cocaine, and methamphetamine. Many people with opioid use disorder now use it knowingly because it has displaced heroin.

## Legal status

US Schedule II for fentanyl; analogs Schedule I as a class.

## What it feels like

### The character of its euphoria

People describe illicit fentanyl as a fast, heavy, short high, with less of heroin's warm, dreamy euphoria and more blank sedation, followed by quick, harsh withdrawal a few hours later. Many describe it as 'chasing', needing to use again and again through the day.

Because pressed pills and powders are mixed unevenly ('chocolate chip cookie effect'), one part may contain little and another a deadly amount. Xylazine adds very long blackouts and skin wounds.

Tolerance changes everything below. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (seconds)

The first sign is often subtle: a faint warmth in the stomach or chest, a slight heaviness behind the eyes, and a loosening of tension in the shoulders. People describe it as the edge coming off whatever they were feeling. With Illicit fentanyl, many say they notice their breathing slow slightly and their thoughts get quieter before they feel anything they would call pleasure.

### Come-up (seconds)

The warmth spreads outward to the arms, legs, and face. Pain, if present, fades into the background rather than disappearing outright; people describe still knowing it is there but no longer caring. A calm, contented mood rises. Itching, usually mild, often starts on the nose, face, or upper body, caused by histamine release and by opioid effects on the nervous system. Some people feel a wave of nausea at this point, especially if they are new to opioids or moving around.

### Peak (1-3 min)

At the peak, people describe a deep, cozy contentment, as if wrapped in a warm blanket, with worries feeling far away and unimportant. Body feels heavy and relaxed; speech can slow. Pupils shrink to pinpoints. People often lose consciousness quickly; many survivors say they never felt it coming. This is also when breathing is most suppressed and overdose is most likely, often without the person noticing anything wrong.

### Plateau (5-20 min)

The effects level off into a steady, calm state. "Nodding" (heavy and sudden with Illicit fentanyl) can occur: drifting in and out of a light sleep, head dropping, then jerking awake, sometimes mid-sentence or with a cigarette or drink in hand. People report vivid, dreamlike half-sleep. Talkativeness or a quiet, inward contentment are both common. Mouth dryness, flushed skin, and difficulty urinating can appear.

### Comedown (20-60 min)

The glow fades gradually. People describe the world getting "louder" again: light, noise, and small problems regain their weight. Tiredness and a flat, slightly gray mood are common. Pain, if it was being treated, returns. In people with dependence, this is where restlessness, yawning, and craving start.

### After-effects (1-4 h)

Short duration often means withdrawal starts within hours, with restlessness and sweats.

### Next day (withdrawal may begin)

After a single medical exposure, most people feel close to normal, perhaps a little foggy or constipated. After repeated use, the next day can bring early withdrawal: anxiety, runny nose, goosebumps, aches, diarrhea, and strong craving. Tolerance builds quickly, which means the same effect needs more drug and overdose risk rises, especially after a break, when tolerance drops but habits do not.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Inhaled and smoked](../routes/inhaled-smoked/fentanyl-illicit.md): Smoking on foil is now the most common way illicit fentanyl is used in many areas.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/fentanyl-illicit.md): Injected, often mixed with other drugs.
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/fentanyl-illicit.md): Snorted, often unknowingly in powders sold as other drugs.
- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/fentanyl-illicit.md): Swallowed as counterfeit pills.

Not covered: No rectal, transdermal, or sublingual illicit use is common enough to cover.

## Overdose note for Illicit fentanyl

Overdose often comes within seconds to minutes, sometimes before the person can put the drug down. Chest-wall rigidity ('wooden chest') can make breathing impossible. Multiple naloxone doses may be needed. Fentanyl test strips can detect it in drugs before use, and are legal in most US states. Never use alone; services like the Never Use Alone hotline (1-877-696-1996) send help if a caller stops responding.

Read the shared page: [What an overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
