# Buprenorphine (Subutex, Suboxone)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic opioid made from thebaine; a partial mu-opioid agonist with very strong receptor binding and a 'ceiling effect' on breathing suppression.

**Brand and street names:** Subutex (buprenorphine alone, brand discontinued in US, generics remain), Suboxone (with naloxone), Zubsolv, Bunavail (discontinued), Sublocade and Brixadi (monthly/weekly injections), Probuphine (implant, discontinued), Butrans (patch, pain), Belbuca (buccal film, pain), Buprenex (injection, pain).

**Brand and combination product pages:** [Suboxone](../products/suboxone.md), [Zubsolv](../products/zubsolv.md), [Subutex (buprenorphine alone)](../products/subutex.md).

## History and origins

Developed by Reckitt & Colman (now Indivior) in Britain, first made in 1966 by John Lewis's team, and launched as an injectable painkiller (Temgesic) in 1978. Research in the 1970s and 1980s showed it could treat opioid addiction. The US Drug Addiction Treatment Act of 2000 let doctors prescribe it for addiction from an office, and Subutex and Suboxone were approved in 2002. In 2023 the special 'X-waiver' was removed, so any clinician with a standard DEA registration can prescribe it.

## Medical use, then and now

Opioid use disorder treatment, and chronic pain (patches, buccal films, injections).

## Role in addiction treatment

Buprenorphine is one of three FDA-approved medications for opioid use disorder, and the one most available outside clinics: it can be prescribed in a doctor's office or by telehealth and picked up at a pharmacy. Its strong grip on opioid receptors blocks other opioids from working fully and stops craving and withdrawal. Starting it too early, while another opioid is still active, can cause 'precipitated withdrawal', a sudden severe sickness, which is why patients are told to wait for mild withdrawal first or use newer low-dose starting methods under a clinician. Suboxone adds naloxone to discourage injection. Like methadone, it roughly halves the risk of death while people stay in treatment.

## How it entered recreational use

Diverted buprenorphine is mostly used to self-treat withdrawal rather than to get high. In some countries, including France and Finland, Subutex injection became a major problem in the 2000s.

## Legal status

US Schedule III. UK Class C.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Buprenorphine's long-acting forms include the Butrans patch (7 days), Sublocade (monthly injection), and Brixadi (weekly or monthly injection), all designed to release steadily without peaks.

## What the pills feel like

Suboxone film (DailyMed: an orange rectangular film with a printed logo) is a thin, flexible rectangle, about the size of a postage stamp, that sticks to the mouth lining as it dissolves; each film comes in its own child-resistant foil pouch you tear open. Generic buprenorphine and buprenorphine-naloxone tablets are small, flat, and either round or oval, dissolving under the tongue with a gritty, bitter feel. Zubsolv tablets are small with a menthol taste. Butrans is a square-ish skin patch.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

For people without opioid tolerance, buprenorphine can be surprisingly strong: a long, warm, mellow, slightly spacey euphoria with heavy nausea and sleepiness, often lasting a day or more. For people with tolerance, it produces little euphoria; instead people describe relief, clear-headedness, and feeling "normal." Many in recovery value exactly that flatness.

Because it is a partial agonist with a ceiling, the high does not keep climbing the way full agonists do. Users often describe it as "cleaner" and more energetic than methadone. Nausea and headaches are common at first.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the sublingual and buccal (under the tongue or in the cheek) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (40-80 min)

Under the tongue, buprenorphine dissolves over several minutes with a bitter or flavored taste. Effects start around 40 to 80 minutes. In someone in withdrawal at the right time, the first feeling is relief.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Buprenorphine is metabolized by CYP3A4 to norbuprenorphine; it binds receptors very tightly and has a half-life of about 24 to 42 hours. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (1-2 h)

In people without tolerance, the come-up is surprisingly strong: warm, floaty, nauseous, and sleepy. In people with tolerance, it is mostly the steady disappearance of craving and sickness.

### Peak (1.5-4 h)

The peak, roughly 1 to 4 hours, is a long, mellow high in non-tolerant people, often with heavy nausea. Because of the ceiling effect, more drug does not keep adding euphoria or breathing suppression the same way. Tolerant people describe clear-headedness and feeling 'normal.'

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (4-18 h)

The plateau lasts a very long time, often a full day. People describe being calm and functional, sometimes slightly energized, sometimes with headache.

### Comedown (18-48 h)

The effect fades over 24 to 72 hours, so there is usually no noticeable comedown between daily treatment doses.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (24-72 h)

Headache, constipation, sweating, and insomnia are common. Precipitated withdrawal, if it is taken while a full agonist is still active, feels like sudden severe sickness: vomiting, diarrhea, sweats, and agitation.

### Next day (still active)

Still active the next day and beyond. Withdrawal after stopping is slow to start (often 2 to 4 days) and milder but longer than with full agonists.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

Because of its ceiling effect, higher doses of buprenorphine mainly last longer rather than producing ever-stronger euphoria or breathing suppression. The danger rises sharply when it is combined with benzodiazepines or alcohol, and in children or people without tolerance.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Sublingual and buccal (under the tongue or in the cheek)](../routes/sublingual-buccal/buprenorphine.md): Subutex and Suboxone tablets and films under the tongue; Belbuca buccal film; Zubsolv. The main route.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/buprenorphine.md): Buprenex injection for pain; Sublocade and Brixadi monthly or weekly injections under the skin for addiction; also misused by IV injection.
- [Transdermal (skin patches)](../routes/transdermal/buprenorphine.md): Butrans patch for chronic pain.
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/buprenorphine.md): Misused by snorting; absorption is faster than sublingual.

Not covered: Swallowed buprenorphine is poorly absorbed, so oral is not a medical route. No rectal or smoked forms.

## Overdose note for Buprenorphine (Subutex, Suboxone)

Its ceiling effect makes overdose from buprenorphine alone much less likely in adults, but combining it with benzodiazepines, alcohol, or other sedatives has caused many deaths. Children who swallow films or tablets can die. Naloxone still helps, though higher amounts may be needed because buprenorphine binds so tightly. Precipitated withdrawal is not an overdose, but it is severe.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
