# Cyclobenzaprine (Flexeril)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Muscle relaxant chemically related to tricyclic antidepressants.

**Brand and street names:** Flexeril, Fexmid, Amrix (extended-release capsule).

## History and origins

Approved in 1977 and developed by Merck; it is structurally almost identical to the antidepressant amitriptyline.

## Medical use, then and now

Short-term muscle spasm; used off-label for fibromyalgia and sleep.

## How it entered recreational use

Occasionally misused for sedation or to add to other drugs; not strongly euphoric.

## Legal status

Prescription-only, not federally controlled in the US.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Immediate-release cyclobenzaprine tablets are taken several times a day. Amrix is an extended-release capsule taken once daily, with a slower rise, steadier sedation, and a long tail into the next day; capsules should be swallowed whole.

## What the pills feel like

Generic immediate-release tablets are small, round, and film-coated, so they feel smooth. Amrix comes as capsules.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Cyclobenzaprine's character is heavy, sticky sleepiness with a dry mouth, rather than a high. Many people find it unpleasant: groggy, foggy and hungover.

Its tricyclic structure gives anticholinergic effects: dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, and a fast heartbeat.

Combined with SSRIs, tramadol or MAO inhibitors it can contribute to serotonin syndrome, and in overdose it can disturb heart rhythm.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (about 1 h)

Cyclobenzaprine (Flexeril) is chemically a close cousin of the tricyclic antidepressant amitriptyline, and it feels like one. The onset is a slow, thick drowsiness: the eyelids grow heavy, the mouth turns noticeably dry and sticky, and the body begins to feel sluggish rather than euphoric.

Mentally the first change is a dulling of alertness. Thoughts slow, the urge to do things fades, and a vague, cotton-wool feeling settles over the mind. There is usually little pleasure in it; most people simply feel sleepy.

Anticholinergic signs appear: dry eyes, slightly blurry near vision, a faster heartbeat, and sometimes difficulty starting to urinate. A strange metallic or bitter aftertaste is common.

Medically, the onset reflects absorption from the gut and entry into the brain. Cyclobenzaprine is processed by CYP3A4, CYP1A2 and CYP2D6 and has a long half-life, longer in older adults. Muscle relaxants work mostly through central sedation rather than acting on the muscles themselves (dantrolene is the exception), so drowsiness usually arrives together with the loosening of tension.

Clinicians warn that reaction time slows early, often before the person notices, and that dizziness on standing is common, especially in older adults.

### Come-up (1-2 h)

Dry mouth and heaviness grow.

### Peak (3-8 h)

At the peak the body is heavy and slow, with muscle spasm eased but not in a warm or glowing way. Dizziness on standing, unsteady walking and a parched mouth dominate. Some feel their heart pounding or skipping while lying still.

Emotionally the peak is muted and foggy. Some report a mild dreamy detachment; others feel uncomfortable, restless or confused. Higher amounts push it toward a deliriant state with odd thoughts, vivid dreams or hallucinations rather than toward euphoria.

Overdose is dangerous mainly because of the heart: fast or irregular rhythms, low blood pressure, agitation, hallucinations and seizures. Mixing with serotonergic antidepressants or tramadol can cause serotonin syndrome, with shaking, sweating, fever and confusion.

Medically, the peak is when sedation and dizziness are strongest. Several muscle relaxants have anticholinergic or blood-pressure effects as well, which can cause dry mouth, blurred vision, a fast or slow heartbeat, or fainting.

Alone they rarely stop breathing in healthy adults, but combined with opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, or gabapentinoids they add to respiratory depression; carisoprodol in particular was a common part of the deadly 'holy trinity' with opioids and benzodiazepines.

### Plateau (several hours)

The plateau is long and sleepy. Many people sleep deeply for hours and wake with a thick head. Those who stay awake feel heavy-limbed, dry-mouthed and slightly disconnected from their body.

Concentration and memory are poor, and reaction time is slow, much as with an older sedating antihistamine.

Because it lasts a long time, drowsiness frequently carries into the next day, making driving risky.

### Comedown (12-24 h)

The comedown is a slow-lifting grogginess, often described as a hangover: dry mouth, headache, and heavy limbs.

Mood can be flat or irritable, and some feel mentally slow most of the next morning.

Cyclobenzaprine is not usually craved, but stopping after long daily use can cause nausea, headache and malaise.

As levels fall, sedation lifts and muscle tension may return. Short-acting drugs wear off within hours, while drugs with active metabolites (carisoprodol to meprobamate) linger.

People who use carisoprodol, meprobamate, or baclofen daily can develop dependence; stopping suddenly can cause anxiety, insomnia, hallucinations, and seizures, and abrupt baclofen withdrawal can be life-threatening.

### After-effects (next day)

Dry mouth and grogginess.

### Next day (morning fog)

Morning hangover common.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, muscle relaxants mainly cause drowsiness and eased tension. At higher doses, dizziness, heavy sedation, poor coordination, blurred vision, and confusion grow, and some (carisoprodol, meprobamate, orphenadrine, baclofen, tizanidine) bring additional dangers: seizures, heart rhythm problems, very low blood pressure, or coma. With opioids, alcohol, or benzodiazepines, breathing can slow and stop.

Higher amounts bring heavier anticholinergic effects: racing heart, confusion, hallucinations, and in overdose heart rhythm problems and seizures.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

## Overdose note for Cyclobenzaprine (Flexeril)

Overdose causes drowsiness, fast heartbeat, agitation, hallucinations, and, at larger amounts, dangerous heart rhythms and seizures, similar to tricyclic antidepressant poisoning.
