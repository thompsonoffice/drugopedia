# Methoxetamine (MXE)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Research-chemical dissociative, a ketamine analog.

**Brand and street names:** MXE, mexxy.

## History and origins

Appeared online around 2010, marketed as a 'bladder-friendly ketamine', a claim not supported. Linked to deaths, it was controlled in the UK in 2012 and added to UN Schedule II in 2016.

## Medical use, then and now

None.

## How it entered recreational use

Sold online as a research chemical in the early 2010s.

## Legal status

US Schedule I (2022); UN 1971 Convention Schedule II.

## What the products look and feel like

White powder. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

MXE was slower and longer than ketamine, with more mood lift and stimulation; deep dissociation could arrive late.

The delayed onset led people to take more. Reported harms include agitation, high blood pressure, fast heart rate, confusion and catatonia.

Bladder damage was reported despite the marketing.

Tolerance to dissociatives builds quickly, and ketamine tolerance leads people to use more, raising the risk of bladder and memory damage.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (10-30 min)

Methoxetamine (MXE) is a designer dissociative related to ketamine, sold as a research chemical from around 2010 until bans in many countries. It comes on more slowly than ketamine, often taking a while to build, which leads some people to redose before it has kicked in. Early on there is a light, tingling body buzz, unsteady movement and a warm, loose feeling.

Mentally the onset is a gradual drift into detachment, with a mood lift that many found more euphoric and longer-lasting than ketamine. Music seems richer and thoughts feel interesting.

Delayed onset and strong potency made accidental heavy dosing common, and emergency departments saw people with agitation, confusion and fast heart rates.

MXE was designed as a ketamine analog with a longer half-life and slower onset.

Raised blood pressure and fast heart rate were common in hospital reports.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

Gradual detachment.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At its peak the body is heavy or numb, coordination is poor and walking difficult. Heart rate and blood pressure often rise, unlike pure sedatives. Eyes may flick involuntarily and speech slur, and some people sweat or feel flushed.

Mentally there can be deep dissociation: the self and the room fragment, time distorts, and some enter a 'hole' state of profound disconnection with dreamlike visions. Some people feel euphoric, others frightened or paranoid. Some feel a strong sense of floating or flying through space.

Risks include falls, choking on vomit, high blood pressure, agitation and accidents. Mixing with alcohol or depressants raises the danger of breathing problems.

At peak, deep dissociation, agitation, cerebellar signs (staggering, poor coordination) and catatonia were reported.

Deaths usually involved other drugs.

### Plateau (2-3 h)

The plateau is long, several hours, with the body heavy and clumsy, the eyes sometimes flicking side to side, and nausea possible. Walking stays difficult, and some people feel tingling or numbness in their hands and feet. Thirst and a racing heart may persist.

Mentally many feel calm, thoughtful and detached, sometimes with mood lifting that lingers. Others feel stuck in confusing, looping thoughts. Some notice a warm, reflective mood and like to lie still with music; others feel restless and talkative.

Bladder damage, similar to ketamine's, and long-term memory problems are concerns with frequent use. Accidents, falls and getting lost are risks while coordination is impaired.

### Comedown (3-5 h)

The comedown can be slow, with tiredness, headache and lingering unsteadiness. Sleep may be hard to reach and broken, muscles may ache, and the head can feel thick and slow the next day. Some feel nauseated and lightheaded when standing.

Mentally some feel low, flat or anxious, while others feel brighter for a day. Frequent use has been linked to depression, dependence and psychosis. Some feel irritable, tearful or detached from others for several days, and memory and concentration can be poor.

Avoid driving until fully clear, and seek help for bladder pain or blood in urine. Support from mental-health services can help with low mood and craving.

MXE effects last several hours, longer than ketamine.

Bladder toxicity was demonstrated in animal studies, contrary to its marketing.

### After-effects (5-12 h)

Low mood and fatigue.

### Next day

Next-day fog.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses dissociatives bring numbness, lightness, mild euphoria and dreamy detachment. At higher doses, the body feels far away, movement becomes difficult, and vision and sound distort. At still higher levels, people lose contact with surroundings entirely, cannot move or respond, and are at risk of injury, vomiting and choking, with breathing danger when combined with depressants.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

Emergencies include agitation, high blood pressure, seizures and catatonia; deaths often involved other drugs.
