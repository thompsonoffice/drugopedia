# Zaleplon (Sonata)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Ultra-short-acting Z-drug hypnotic (pyrazolopyrimidine).

**Brand and street names:** Sonata, Starnoc and Andante (outside US).

## History and origins

Approved in 1999. Discontinued as a brand in the US but available as generics.

## Medical use, then and now

Trouble falling asleep, including middle-of-the-night use.

## How it entered recreational use

Little misuse; occasionally taken for brief hallucinatory effects.

## Legal status

US Schedule IV.

## What the pills feel like

Capsules.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Zaleplon's character is a fast, brief sleepiness that leaves the body within a few hours.

If the person stays awake, short-lived visual distortions and amnesia can occur.

Its short action means little next-day grogginess.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (15-20 min)

Zaleplon (Sonata) is the fastest and shortest of the Z-drugs, often felt within a quarter of an hour. The body grows heavy and warm, the shoulders and jaw let go, and a soft, swimmy dizziness rises into the head. Lying down in the dark, it feels like being gently pulled under, as if sleep had been switched on.

Mentally a quick, dreamy drowsiness takes over. Worries fade and thoughts loosen and drift. If the person fights sleep, a brief zolpidem-like giddiness can appear, with a silly mood, lowered inhibitions and odd perceptions such as surfaces shimmering or patterns sliding on the ceiling.

Memory for the period after taking it is often poor, sometimes starting almost immediately, so people may not recall the last few minutes before sleep.

Medically, the onset reflects absorption and entry into the brain. Zaleplon is processed by aldehyde oxidase and CYP3A4, with a half-life of about 1 hour. Most hypnotics are designed to work quickly so the person falls asleep soon after taking them.

Clinicians advise taking hypnotics only right before bed, because people who stay awake can experience confusion, hallucinations, and memory loss.

### Come-up (20-30 min)

Fast heaviness.

### Peak (about 1 h)

At the peak, the pull toward sleep is strong and most people are asleep. Those still awake are unsteady, slurring and foggy, with heavy eyelids and clumsy hands.

Emotionally the peak is mellow or mildly disinhibited, and mild visual distortions — rippling patterns or shapes at the edge of vision — can occur.

Z-drugs rarely stop breathing on their own, but combined with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids they can cause dangerous sedation and slowed breathing. Complex sleep behaviors, such as sleep-driving, cooking or walking outside with no memory, carry an FDA boxed warning. Anyone who cannot be woken, or is vomiting while drowsy, needs emergency help.

Medically, the peak is when sleep pressure is strongest. People who resist sleep at this point may walk, eat, talk, drive, or send messages with no memory afterward ('complex sleep behaviors'), which led to a 2019 FDA boxed warning for zolpidem, zaleplon, and eszopiclone.

Combined with alcohol, opioids, or other sedatives, breathing can slow dangerously, and amnesia is much more likely.

### Plateau (1-2 h)

The zaleplon plateau is brief, often under a couple of hours, and usually spent asleep. Sleep tends to be deep at first, then lightens as the drug clears, which is why some people wake in the early morning hours. The body feels heavy and still, with slow, regular breathing.

If awake, thinking is fragmented, short-term memory poor, and movements clumsy. People may get up for water or the bathroom and remember none of it, or have half-dreaming conversations they cannot recall.

Because it clears quickly, next-day grogginess is less common than with other sleep drugs, which is why it is sometimes prescribed for middle-of-the-night waking when enough hours remain before getting up.

### Comedown (3-4 h total)

Waking after zaleplon is usually fairly clear. Some people feel slightly heavy, dry-mouthed or headachy for the first hour, but most report feeling close to normal, without the drugged hangover of longer sleep medicines.

Mood is generally steady, though some feel anxious or uneasy about patchy memory from the night before, and a few notice a slightly flat or irritable mood in the morning.

Tolerance and rebound insomnia can develop with regular use, with a night or two of poor sleep after stopping, and dependence is possible. Like other Z-drugs it carries a boxed warning for complex sleep behaviors. Taking it with too little time before waking can leave people foggy.

As levels fall, most people sleep through the comedown. Those who wake early can feel groggy, unsteady, and slow, and next-morning impairment can affect driving, especially in women and older adults.

Nightly use can cause tolerance and rebound insomnia on stopping; some hypnotics (GHB, methaqualone, older drugs) cause severe dependence and dangerous withdrawal.

### After-effects (4-6 h)

Little grogginess.

### Next day (usually none)

Usually no next-day effect.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, hypnotics bring sleepiness and calm. At higher doses, or when the person stays awake, confusion, memory loss, hallucinations, and sleep-walking or sleep-driving become likely. With alcohol or opioids, or with drugs like GHB and methaqualone, effects can tip into unconsciousness, vomiting while unconscious, and stopped breathing.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

## Overdose note for Zaleplon (Sonata)

Overdose causes deep sleep; with other depressants breathing can slow.
