# Methyprylon (Noludar)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Older non-barbiturate sedative-hypnotic.

**Brand and street names:** Noludar.

## History and origins

Introduced by Roche in the 1950s; withdrawn in the 1990s.

## Medical use, then and now

Formerly insomnia; no longer used.

## How it entered recreational use

Misused in the mid to late 20th century.

## Legal status

US Schedule II or IV; withdrawn from the market.

## What the pills feel like

Historic products only.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Methyprylon's character was a barbiturate-like sedation and sleep.

Its safety margin was narrow, and overdose and dependence were common.

It is now historical.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (about 45 min)

Methyprylon (Noludar), a sleep drug from the 1950s now discontinued, brought a gradual, heavy, barbiturate-like drowsiness over about half an hour. The limbs grew slow and weighted, the head heavy, and the eyelids difficult to keep open, while a mild warmth spread through the body. The face could flush slightly and the hands feel numb.

Mentally it brought a calm, sleepy fog with slowed thoughts and fading anxiety, sometimes with a slight drunk-like looseness before sleep took over.

Coordination slowed, speech softened and became slurred, and some people felt dizzy or nauseated. It was promoted as a non-barbiturate, but felt much the same. It acted within an hour.

Medically, the onset reflects absorption and entry into the brain. Methyprylon was processed by the liver. Most hypnotics are designed to work quickly so the person falls asleep soon after taking them.

Clinicians advise taking hypnotics only right before bed, because people who stay awake can experience confusion, hallucinations, and memory loss.

### Come-up (1 h)

Drowsiness.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At the peak there was strong sedation, clumsiness and a powerful pull toward sleep. The body felt heavy and numb, and most people fell asleep quickly and deeply. Muscles were slack and breathing slowed. The face could feel numb, eyes heavy and unfocused, and fine movements such as buttoning or writing became fumbling and slow.

Emotionally it was dull and drowsy, with memory gaps and little awareness of time. Some people became confused or briefly excited before sleep, particularly older adults.

Overdose caused coma, low blood pressure, slowed breathing and sometimes shock, similar to barbiturate poisoning, and was more dangerous with alcohol. Reflexes slowed.

Medically, the peak is when sleep pressure is strongest. People who resist sleep at this point may walk, eat, talk, drive, or send messages with no memory afterward ('complex sleep behaviors'), which led to a 2019 FDA boxed warning for zolpidem, zaleplon, and eszopiclone.

Combined with alcohol, opioids, or other sedatives, breathing can slow dangerously, and amnesia is much more likely.

### Plateau (sleep)

The methyprylon plateau was sleep for most of the night, deep and heavy, with slow breathing and little movement. People were difficult to wake. The skin could feel cool, and breathing was slow and regular, sometimes with loud snoring. People who rose in the night often stumbled or fell, and older users were particularly prone to confusion and injury.

If awake, thinking was slow, confused and forgetful, speech slurred and balance poor. Some people became restless, confused or agitated instead of sleepy, especially in the elderly.

Alcohol made it far more dangerous, and nightly use built tolerance, leading some people to take more and risk overdose.

### Comedown (5-8 h)

The comedown was a hangover with heaviness, grogginess, headache and sometimes nausea, often carrying into the morning. Coordination and reaction time stayed impaired for hours, making morning driving risky.

Mood was flat and slow, with irritability or mild depression in some regular users. Some regular users woke with shaky hands and a dry, sour mouth, feeling restless and on edge until they took another dose, an early sign of dependence that could escalate.

Dependence occurred with regular use, with barbiturate-like withdrawal including anxiety, tremor, insomnia, hallucinations and seizures. It was eventually withdrawn as safer drugs appeared.

As levels fall, most people sleep through the comedown. Those who wake early can feel groggy, unsteady, and slow, and next-morning impairment can affect driving, especially in women and older adults.

Nightly use can cause tolerance and rebound insomnia on stopping; some hypnotics (GHB, methaqualone, older drugs) cause severe dependence and dangerous withdrawal.

### After-effects (8-24 h)

Grogginess.

### Next day (hangover)

Hangover.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, hypnotics bring sleepiness and calm. At higher doses, or when the person stays awake, confusion, memory loss, hallucinations, and sleep-walking or sleep-driving become likely. With alcohol or opioids, or with drugs like GHB and methaqualone, effects can tip into unconsciousness, vomiting while unconscious, and stopped breathing.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

Overdose caused deep coma, low blood pressure, and slowed breathing that could last for days.
