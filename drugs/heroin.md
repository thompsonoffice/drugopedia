# Heroin (diacetylmorphine)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic opioid made from morphine; a prodrug that becomes 6-monoacetylmorphine and morphine in the body, and enters the brain faster than morphine.

**Brand and street names:** Heroin (Bayer, 1898 to about 1913); Diamorphine (UK medical use).

## History and origins

C.R. Alder Wright first made diacetylmorphine in London in 1874. In 1897 Felix Hoffmann at Bayer (who also made aspirin) re-made it, and Bayer sold it from 1898 as 'Heroin' (from German 'heroisch', heroic), a cough medicine and supposedly non-addictive morphine substitute, even for children. Within a few years its addictiveness was obvious; Bayer stopped making it around 1913, the US Harrison Act of 1914 restricted it, and it was banned in the US in 1924. Heroin waves followed in the 1950s to 1970s (including among Vietnam veterans) and again from the 2000s, when people with prescription opioid addiction switched to cheaper heroin. Since about 2013, the US heroin supply has been largely replaced or contaminated by fentanyl.

## Medical use, then and now

As diamorphine, used in the UK and some other countries for severe pain, heart attacks, and palliative care. Switzerland, the Netherlands, Germany, Denmark, and Canada run heroin-assisted treatment for people for whom other treatments have not worked.

## How it entered recreational use

One of the most well-known drugs of misuse in the world, linked with jazz and rock subcultures and later with the overdose crisis.

## Legal status

US Schedule I (no accepted medical use). UK Class A, but prescribable as diamorphine.

## What the pills feel like

Forms vary by country and manufacturer; see the brand list above. Ask a pharmacist to describe the product you have.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Heroin is often described as the deepest, warmest, most complete opioid euphoria: a body-wide warmth and safety, "like being hugged by God" or "returning to the womb", with all pain, anxiety, and loneliness gone. Compared with morphine it arrives faster and feels sharper; compared with fentanyl it is warmer, dreamier, and longer.

Writers like Thomas De Quincey (on opium), William Burroughs, and many people in recovery have described how this feeling becomes the center of life, and then turns into the daily need to avoid withdrawal. Today, almost all street 'heroin' in North America contains fentanyl, so the experience and risk are no longer what they were.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (IV 0-5 sec; IM/SC 5-15 min)

Heroin's onset depends heavily on route, but in every case it is faster and more abrupt than pills. When it is smoked or snorted, the first signs come within minutes: a sudden warmth flooding the chest and stomach, a heaviness in the limbs, and an immediate loosening of tension. Many people describe the onset as 'a wave' or 'a warm hug from the inside'.

Mentally, the onset is a fast, intense surge of relief and pleasure. Anxiety, physical pain and emotional pain recede together, and people often describe feeling as if all their problems simply stop mattering. This combination of speed and emotional relief is central to heroin's strong pull.

Physical signs follow quickly: dry mouth, a flushed face, pinpoint pupils, and frequently a warm, prickly itch. Nausea and vomiting are common, especially in people new to opioids. Today's street heroin is very often mixed with or replaced by fentanyl and sometimes xylazine, which makes the onset unpredictable and dangerous.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Heroin crosses into the brain faster than morphine and is turned within minutes into 6-monoacetylmorphine and then morphine, which carry the effect; heroin itself lasts only minutes in the blood. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (IV seconds)

Injected, the rush is described as overwhelming: a hot flush, a sense of release, heavy limbs, and sometimes nausea or vomiting. Smoked or snorted, it is gentler and builds over minutes.

### Peak (IV 1-5 min)

The peak is a deep, heavy, all-over warmth. The limbs feel extremely heavy, as if sinking, and the skin tingles and itches. Breathing slows and becomes shallow, and the heart slows. Vision can become soft and unfocused, and the eyes close easily.

The emotional tone is often described as profound comfort and safety, a sense of being cocooned, rather than excitement. Thoughts slow down and become dream-like. Many people report feeling detached from everything, peaceful and indifferent to worries.

The peak is the most dangerous point. Breathing can slow to the point of stopping, and fentanyl contamination makes this far more likely. Signs of overdose include being unable to wake, very slow or stopped breathing, gurgling or snoring sounds, and blue or gray lips. Naloxone and calling 911 save lives.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (1-4 h)

The plateau is marked by nodding: drifting into a light sleep, chin dropping, then jerking awake. The body feels heavy and warm, and itching continues. Sweating and a flushed face are common. Speech can be slow and slurred.

Mentally, this phase is drowsy and dream-like. People drift between waking and dreaming, and time feels very elastic. The emotional tone stays calm and detached but becomes sleepier.

Constipation, nausea and difficulty urinating become noticeable. Breathing is still depressed, and people who nod off, especially after mixing with alcohol or benzodiazepines, are at serious risk.

### Comedown (4-5 h total)

As heroin wears off, the warmth fades fairly quickly. People describe the body feeling heavy, then tired and achy. Itch subsides, and noise and light can feel harsher.

Emotionally, the comedown often brings a low, flat mood and restlessness. For people who use regularly, the end of the effect is quickly followed by craving and anxiety.

In dependent people, withdrawal symptoms can begin within hours: yawning, sweating, runny nose, goosebumps, stomach cramps and intense craving. Using again during this phase, especially when tolerance has dropped, is a common cause of overdose.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (5-12 h)

Constipation, dry mouth, and fatigue. With street supply now dominated by fentanyl, duration and intensity are unpredictable.

### Next day

After regular use, withdrawal begins 6 to 12 hours after last use and peaks at 1 to 3 days: sweats, chills, goosebumps, aches, diarrhea, vomiting, restless legs, and insomnia.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/heroin.md): IV, IM ('muscling'), and SC ('skin-popping'); medical diamorphine injection in the UK.
- [Inhaled and smoked](../routes/inhaled-smoked/heroin.md): 'Chasing the dragon', smoking vapor from foil.
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/heroin.md): Snorting, common with powder heroin; medical diamorphine nasal spray is used for children's pain in UK emergency departments.
- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/heroin.md): Medical diamorphine tablets exist in the UK; swallowed heroin largely turns into morphine and feels like oral morphine.

Not covered: No transdermal or common rectal use; rectal use exists but is rare and not covered.

## Overdose note for Heroin (diacetylmorphine)

Since street heroin now usually contains fentanyl, overdose risk is unpredictable. Overdoses cluster after time away from use (jail, rehab, hospital), when tolerance is lower.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
