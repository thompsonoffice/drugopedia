# LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic ergoline psychedelic; potent 5-HT2A agonist.

**Brand and street names:** Acid, tabs, blotter, L, Lucy; Delysid (former Sandoz brand).

## History and origins

Albert Hofmann synthesized LSD at Sandoz in 1938 and discovered its effects by accident on April 16, 1943, taking it deliberately three days later ('Bicycle Day'). Sandoz supplied it as Delysid for psychiatric research. The CIA tested it in MKUltra. Recreational use exploded in the 1960s counterculture, leading to bans in 1966-1970.

## Medical use, then and now

Used in psychiatric research in the 1950s and 1960s; now in clinical trials for anxiety (an LSD formulation, MM120, received FDA breakthrough designation for anxiety in 2024).

## How it entered recreational use

Spread through the 1960s counterculture, music and festival scenes, and remains common.

## Legal status

US Schedule I; UN 1971 Convention Schedule I.

## What the products look and feel like

Blotter paper squares, often printed with art; sometimes liquid or small gelatin squares. A bitter or numbing taste suggests an NBOME, not LSD. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

LSD's character is long, clear and 'electric': vivid colors, patterns breathing on surfaces, deep emotional and philosophical thinking, and a stimulating energy.

Time stretches over a very long experience; thoughts can loop, and people may confront difficult memories ('bad trip'), which is the main danger alongside risky behavior.

LSD has very low physical toxicity, but it can trigger lasting psychosis in vulnerable people, and blotters sold as LSD sometimes contain NBOMes, which are far more dangerous.

Tolerance to classic psychedelics builds within a day and lasts several days, with cross-tolerance between them.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (30-90 min)

LSD's onset is slow and subtle. People first notice a restless, slightly anxious energy, yawning, goosebumps or chills, and dilated pupils. Some feel mild nausea or a tight jaw.

Mentally, there is a growing sense that something is shifting: colors seem brighter, objects look slightly alive, and thoughts become associative.

Heart rate and blood pressure rise modestly, and body temperature may increase.

LSD is absorbed from the mouth and gut; very small amounts are active, which is why blotters can carry it.

Pupils dilate and heart rate and temperature rise slightly; some people feel nauseated or cold.

### Come-up (1-2 h)

Over the next hour colors brighten and patterns appear; emotions intensify.

### Peak (3-5 h)

At the peak, visual changes are prominent: surfaces breathe, patterns flow, and colors intensify. The body can feel energized, tingly or heavy.

The emotional tone can be awe, joy and insight, or anxiety and confusion. Time can feel stretched or meaningless, and sense of self may dissolve.

Risks include panic, frightening experiences and dangerous behavior. Blotters sold as LSD may contain NBOMes.

At the peak, 5-HT2A activation in the cortex and thalamus loosens the brain's normal filtering, producing hallucination-like visual effects and altered sense of self.

Physical toxicity is very low, but panic, confusion and accidents are real risks, and people with personal or family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder are at higher risk of lasting harm.

### Plateau (5-8 h)

The plateau is long: visuals and energy continue for hours.

Mentally, people may reflect deeply or feel overwhelmed.

Sleep is difficult until the effects fade.

### Comedown (8-12 h)

The comedown brings tiredness while visual effects soften.

Emotionally, people feel reflective or drained.

Some experience lingering anxiety or, rarely, persistent perception changes (HPPD).

LSD's half-life is about 3 to 4 hours, but its receptor binding keeps effects going far longer.

A minority report hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD): lasting visual trails or static.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Difficulty sleeping and a reflective afterglow are common.

### Next day

Tiredness the next day; tolerance lasts several days.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses psychedelics bring brighter colors, mood changes and new ways of thinking. At higher doses, strong visual changes, loss of the usual sense of self, and time distortion occur. At very high levels, people may lose touch with reality entirely, and panic, confusion and risky behavior become more likely.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: body size, tolerance, genetics, mood and setting, health conditions, medicines (especially antidepressants and lithium), and product purity all change the effect.

## Overdose note for LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide)

There is no known human death from LSD toxicity alone at typical levels; deaths result from accidents, suicide, or substances sold as LSD (especially NBOMes).
