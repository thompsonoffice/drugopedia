# Meprobamate (Miltown)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Carbamate anxiolytic and sedative; the active metabolite of carisoprodol.

**Brand and street names:** Miltown, Equanil, Equagesic (with aspirin).

## History and origins

Developed by Frank Berger and Bernard Ludwig and launched in 1955 as Miltown, it was the first blockbuster 'tranquilizer', fueling 1950s celebrity use and Hollywood jokes. It was replaced by benzodiazepines in the 1960s.

## Medical use, then and now

Anxiety (now rarely prescribed). Withdrawn in Europe in 2012.

## How it entered recreational use

Widely misused in its heyday; dependence and overdose were common.

## Legal status

US Schedule IV. Withdrawn across the EU.

## What the pills feel like

Historic Miltown tablets were white and round.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Meprobamate's character is a barbiturate-like calm: warm, drunk-like relaxation, loosened anxiety, and clumsiness.

Tolerance and dependence develop quickly; withdrawal can cause seizures.

Its safety margin is narrower than benzodiazepines.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (about 1 h)

Meprobamate (Miltown, Equanil), the 1950s 'peace pill', comes on smoothly over an hour or so. A warm heaviness settles across the shoulders and chest, the jaw unclenches, and tension seems to drain down out of the body. A mild, tipsy looseness begins, similar to the first drink of an evening but softer.

Mentally the onset is a gentle, worry-free calm. Anxious thoughts lose their urgency, social unease melts, and people feel easygoing and unbothered. Its effect is closer to a barbiturate or alcohol than to modern anti-anxiety pills, with a slightly fuzzy, contented quality.

Speech slows a little, the eyelids droop, and coordination starts to slip. Some people feel mildly dizzy or nauseated.

Medically, the onset reflects absorption from the gut and entry into the brain. Meprobamate is cleared by the liver with a half-life of about 10 hours, longer with daily use. Muscle relaxants work mostly through central sedation rather than acting on the muscles themselves (dantrolene is the exception), so drowsiness usually arrives together with the loosening of tension.

Clinicians warn that reaction time slows early, often before the person notices, and that dizziness on standing is common, especially in older adults.

### Come-up (1-2 h)

Gradual calm and looseness.

### Peak (1-3 h)

At the peak the body feels heavy, warm and unsteady. Walking is like being tipsy, fine movements are clumsy, and the eyes may flick side to side. Muscles are loose, the shoulders drop, and the urge to lie down is strong. Some feel a tingly numbness in the hands and lips.

Emotionally the peak is a contented, sedated mellowness with lowered inhibitions. People may become chatty and silly or quietly dreamy, and memory for this time can be fuzzy. Judgment is loosened more than the person realizes.

Overdose resembles barbiturate overdose: deep sleep, low blood pressure, slow breathing and coma, sometimes with pill masses in the stomach that delay recovery. Alcohol and opioids make it far more dangerous.

Medically, the peak is when sedation and dizziness are strongest. Several muscle relaxants have anticholinergic or blood-pressure effects as well, which can cause dry mouth, blurred vision, a fast or slow heartbeat, or fainting.

Alone they rarely stop breathing in healthy adults, but combined with opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol, or gabapentinoids they add to respiratory depression; carisoprodol in particular was a common part of the deadly 'holy trinity' with opioids and benzodiazepines.

### Plateau (3-6 h)

The meprobamate plateau is a long, drowsy calm lasting many hours. Many people nap heavily and wake still feeling slowed. Those awake feel sluggish and slightly drunk, with heavy limbs, slurred speech, a dry mouth and a tendency to sway when standing.

Concentration is poor, reaction time slow, and judgment loosened. Conversation may be repetitive or forgetful, and the person may feel content to sit and do nothing. Anxiety stays muffled throughout.

Driving or operating machinery is dangerous throughout this stretch, and adding alcohol or another sedative stacks onto sedation that is still present.

### Comedown (6-12 h total)

The comedown is groggy and slow, with heavy limbs, dry mouth, a dull headache and sometimes nausea, like a mild hangover. Coordination may remain a bit off into the next morning.

Mood may turn flat, irritable or anxious as the calm fades, and that returning anxiety can encourage taking more, which is how dependence builds.

Dependence develops with regular use, and stopping abruptly can cause tremor, insomnia, vomiting, hallucinations and seizures. Withdrawal should be medically supervised.

As levels fall, sedation lifts and muscle tension may return. Short-acting drugs wear off within hours, while drugs with active metabolites (carisoprodol to meprobamate) linger.

People who use carisoprodol, meprobamate, or baclofen daily can develop dependence; stopping suddenly can cause anxiety, insomnia, hallucinations, and seizures, and abrupt baclofen withdrawal can be life-threatening.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Grogginess.

### Next day

Next-day sedation.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, muscle relaxants mainly cause drowsiness and eased tension. At higher doses, dizziness, heavy sedation, poor coordination, blurred vision, and confusion grow, and some (carisoprodol, meprobamate, orphenadrine, baclofen, tizanidine) bring additional dangers: seizures, heart rhythm problems, very low blood pressure, or coma. With opioids, alcohol, or benzodiazepines, breathing can slow and stop.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

## Overdose note for Meprobamate (Miltown)

Overdose causes coma, low blood pressure, slowed breathing, and sometimes tablet clumps (bezoars) in the stomach that keep releasing drug.
