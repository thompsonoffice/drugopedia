# Methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Dopamine and noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor.

**Brand and street names:** Ritalin, Ritalin LA, Concerta, Metadate CD, Methylin, Daytrana patch, Jornay PM, QuilliChew; dexmethylphenidate: Focalin. Street: kiddie coke, vitamin R.

## History and origins

Synthesized by Leandro Panizzon at Ciba in 1944 and named Ritalin after his wife Marguerite, nicknamed Rita; approved in the US in 1955. It became the leading ADHD medicine in the 1990s.

## Medical use, then and now

ADHD and narcolepsy.

## How it entered recreational use

Misused for studying; injecting tablets causes lung damage from fillers.

## Legal status

US Schedule II; UN 1971 Convention Schedule II.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Immediate-release methylphenidate lasts about 3 to 4 hours. Extended-release forms include Concerta (an osmotic tablet whose empty shell may pass in stool), Ritalin LA and Metadate CD (bead capsules), Daytrana (patch) and Jornay PM (taken in the evening). These last about 8 to 12 hours. Damaging them is dangerous.

## What the pills feel like

Per DailyMed, Concerta tablets are capsule-shaped and printed 'alza' with the strength number; IR Ritalin tablets are small and round. Stimulant drugs sold on the street, including counterfeit Adderall, are often contaminated with methamphetamine or fentanyl.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Methylphenidate feels more 'clean' and shorter than amphetamine: alertness and focus, but often more jittery and with a steeper comedown.

Side effects include low appetite, headache, stomach pain, insomnia and raised heart rate.

Misuse can cause anxiety, paranoia and dangerous heart effects.

Tolerance to the euphoria builds quickly, while the heart and blood-pressure effects remain. Regular users often chase the first high and use more often.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (20-60 min)

Methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta, Medikinet) blocks the reuptake of dopamine and noradrenaline. Immediate-release tablets come on within a short while; extended-release forms like Concerta rise in stages over the day. The body perks up gently: a slightly faster heart, a dry mouth, a sense of tension in the shoulders or jaw and fading hunger.

Mentally the onset is a sharpening: the noise of distraction quiets, the urge to fidget fades in people with ADHD, and focus narrows onto the task. People without ADHD may feel a clean, mildly euphoric alertness, often described as 'cleaner' or 'drier' than amphetamine.

Stomachaches and headaches are common early, especially in children.

Methylphenidate blocks dopamine and noradrenaline reuptake rather than releasing them, so its effect depends on ongoing nerve activity.

Heart rate and blood pressure rise modestly.

### Come-up (about 1 h)

Alertness rises.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At its peak the body feels alert and wired, with raised pulse and blood pressure, sweating, and sometimes a tremor or tense muscles. Fingers may feel cold, the stomach unsettled, and some people pick at skin or clench teeth without noticing.

Mentally there is focus and drive; at higher amounts it becomes jittery, anxious, irritable and obsessive, with racing thoughts and less euphoria than amphetamine. People can become absorbed in one thing for hours, struggle to switch tasks, and feel short-tempered when interrupted.

Risks include heart rhythm problems, high blood pressure and, rarely, psychosis. Misusing crushed tablets in other ways causes serious harm because of the fillers.

At peak, high doses can cause anxiety, tremor and psychosis; injected tablets cause talc granulomas in the lungs and eyes.

With alcohol, the liver forms ethylphenidate, raising levels and heart effects.

### Plateau (3-4 h, ER up to 12 h)

Immediate-release tablets give a plateau of a few hours; extended-release keeps a steady level for much of the day. The body stays mildly tense, with low appetite and dry mouth. Some notice a dull headache or tight neck, and they may go hours without eating or drinking.

Mentally attention holds steady, though some feel emotionally flat, rigid or irritable. Children may seem quieter or less playful; adults sometimes describe feeling 'over-focused' or socially stiff.

Doses late in the day disrupt sleep. Blood pressure and pulse stay modestly raised throughout, which matters for anyone with heart problems. Some people also notice a tic or twitch worsen.

### Comedown (4-12 h)

As it fades, people may feel tired, hungry and headachy, sometimes with a sudden drop in energy. Short-acting tablets can wear off abruptly, leaving a heavy, slowed feeling and sometimes a mild headache. Some people also feel a brief dip in blood sugar-like shakiness until they eat.

Emotionally a rebound of irritability, tearfulness or restlessness is common, especially in children in the late afternoon. Teenagers and adults may feel anxious, flat or teary, and ADHD symptoms often return all at once.

Misuse at high amounts can lead to dependence and a crash with low mood and exhaustion. Sleep may be delayed, which carries tiredness into the next day. Extended-release forms usually give a gentler landing.

Short half-life (about 2 to 3 hours) explains the IR rebound.

Long-acting forms reduce rebound but can cause insomnia.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Insomnia and low appetite.

### Next day

Mild next-day fatigue.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses stimulants increase alertness, focus, energy and confidence and reduce appetite. At higher doses euphoria, talkativeness, restlessness, jaw clenching, sweating, a pounding heart and anxiety increase. At very high levels, paranoia, hallucinations, overheating, chest pain, seizures, heart attack and stroke can occur.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta)

Overdose causes agitation, tremor, high fever, fast heart rhythms, seizures and psychosis.
