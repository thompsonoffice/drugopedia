# DMT (N,N-dimethyltryptamine)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Short-acting tryptamine psychedelic found in many plants.

**Brand and street names:** DMT, dimitri, the spirit molecule; changa (smoking blend).

## History and origins

DMT was first synthesized in 1931 by Richard Manske; Stephen Szára discovered its effects in 1956. Rick Strassman's 1990s studies described 'entity' encounters.

## Medical use, then and now

No approved use; studies of IV DMT for depression are ongoing.

## How it entered recreational use

Vaped or smoked for brief, intense 'breakthrough' experiences.

## Legal status

US Schedule I; UN 1971 Schedule I.

## What the products look and feel like

DMT is a white to orange-yellow crystalline powder. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Inhaled DMT is a sudden, overwhelming experience: within seconds, geometric visions and a feeling of leaving one's body, often with apparent encounters with 'beings'.

It is very short, often over within 15 to 20 minutes, which makes it seem less dangerous than it is.

Heart rate and blood pressure spike, and people can fall or drop lit pipes.

Tolerance to classic psychedelics builds within a day and lasts several days, with cross-tolerance between them.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (seconds)

Effects begin within seconds of inhaling.

Inhaled DMT reaches the brain in seconds; swallowed DMT is destroyed by gut MAO and is inactive unless combined with an MAO inhibitor (as in ayahuasca).

Heart rate and blood pressure rise sharply in the first minutes.

### Come-up (30 s-1 min)

A rapid rush of sound and visuals.

### Peak (2-5 min)

Peak at 2 to 5 minutes, with loss of contact with surroundings.

At peak, DMT activates 5-HT2A and sigma-1 receptors; EEG studies show the brain shifting to a dreamlike state.

People with heart disease or high blood pressure face the greatest risk.

### Plateau (5-10 min)

Plateau a few minutes.

### Comedown (10-20 min)

Return over 10 to 20 minutes.

MAO breaks DMT down within minutes, ending effects rapidly.

Afterwards, people may feel shaken; integration of intense experiences can take time.

### After-effects (under 1 h)

Afterglow.

### Next day (none)

Usually none.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses psychedelics bring brighter colors, mood changes and new ways of thinking. At higher doses, strong visual changes, loss of the usual sense of self, and time distortion occur. At very high levels, people may lose touch with reality entirely, and panic, confusion and risky behavior become more likely.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: body size, tolerance, genetics, mood and setting, health conditions, medicines (especially antidepressants and lithium), and product purity all change the effect.

## Overdose note for DMT (N,N-dimethyltryptamine)

Physical toxicity is low, but blood pressure spikes and falls are risks, especially with heart disease.
