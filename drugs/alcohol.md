# Alcohol (ethanol)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Central nervous system depressant; GABA-A enhancer and NMDA blocker.

**Brand and street names:** Beer, wine, spirits, hard seltzers, liqueurs; booze, drink.

## History and origins

Fermented drinks date back at least 9,000 years (residues in pottery from Jiahu, China). Distillation spread in medieval Europe and the Islamic world. The US banned alcohol from 1920 to 1933 (Prohibition). It remains the most widely used intoxicant and causes about 178,000 US deaths a year (CDC, 2020-2021).

## Medical use, then and now

Historically a medicine and antiseptic; today used medically only as an antidote for methanol and ethylene glycol poisoning (largely replaced by fomepizole) and to destroy tissue in certain procedures.

## How it entered recreational use

Used socially across most cultures.

## Legal status

Legal for adults (21 in the US); regulated in most countries; banned in some.

## What the products look and feel like

Labels list alcohol by volume (ABV); spirits are far stronger than beer. Counterfeit or homemade spirits can contain methanol, which causes blindness and death.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Alcohol's character is disinhibition: at first relaxation, talkativeness and confidence, then clumsiness, slurring and poor judgment as levels rise.

Memory blackouts can occur while the person still seems awake. Emotions swing; aggression and risk-taking increase.

Alcohol poisoning kills by stopping breathing or by choking on vomit, and mixing with opioids, benzodiazepines or other depressants is a leading cause of overdose death.

Tolerance hides impairment but not organ damage; daily drinking leads to dependence, and stopping suddenly can cause seizures and delirium tremens.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (10-30 min)

Effects begin within 10 to 30 minutes, faster on an empty stomach: warmth and relaxation.

Alcohol is absorbed mostly from the small intestine; food in the stomach slows it, and carbonation speeds it up.

Early effects come from GABA-A enhancement and dopamine release; blood vessels widen, causing warmth and flushing, while body heat is actually lost.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

As blood alcohol rises, talkativeness and confidence increase.

### Peak (30-90 min)

Peak at 30 to 90 minutes after drinking stops, with the greatest impairment.

At high levels, alcohol suppresses the brainstem's breathing center and the gag reflex, so vomiting while unconscious can block the airway.

Blood alcohol can keep rising for 30 to 90 minutes after the last drink, so a person who 'passes out' may still be getting worse.

### Plateau (depends on drinking)

Impairment continues as long as drinking continues.

### Comedown (hours)

Blood alcohol falls at a roughly fixed rate; sleepiness follows.

The liver clears alcohol at a roughly fixed rate; coffee, cold showers and food do not speed this.

Daily heavy drinkers develop dependence; stopping suddenly can cause tremor, hallucinations, seizures and delirium tremens, which can be fatal and need medical detox.

### After-effects (next morning)

Hangover: headache, nausea, dehydration and anxiety.

### Next day

Next-day impaired concentration; daily drinkers may have withdrawal tremor.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower amounts alcohol brings relaxation and talkativeness. At higher amounts, slurred speech, poor balance, poor judgment and memory blackouts follow. At very high levels, vomiting, unconsciousness, slowed breathing and death from alcohol poisoning occur.

At lower blood alcohol, relaxation and talkativeness. Higher levels bring slurring, staggering and blackouts. Very high levels cause vomiting, unconsciousness, slowed breathing, and death; binge drinking (many drinks in a short time) is the main pattern behind alcohol poisoning.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: body size, sex (women reach higher blood alcohol from the same drinks), food, speed of drinking, tolerance, genetics (for example, ALDH2 variants common in East Asian people cause flushing), age, and medicines all change the effect.

## Overdose note for Alcohol (ethanol)

Alcohol poisoning causes vomiting, confusion, slow breathing, low temperature, seizures and death.
