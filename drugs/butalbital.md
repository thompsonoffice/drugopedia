# Butalbital (Fioricet, Fiorinal)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Intermediate-acting barbiturate, found only in combination products.

**Brand and street names:** Fioricet (with acetaminophen and caffeine), Fiorinal (with aspirin and caffeine), Esgic, Bupap, Allzital; versions with codeine.

**Brand and combination product pages:** [Fioricet and Fiorinal (without codeine)](../products/fioricet.md).

## History and origins

Combined with analgesics since the 1950s for tension headaches. Most butalbital products are exempted from federal scheduling, though Fiorinal is Schedule III.

## Medical use, then and now

Tension headaches. Guidelines discourage it because of rebound headaches and dependence.

## How it entered recreational use

Misused for its drunk-like calm.

## Legal status

Fiorinal Schedule III; most acetaminophen versions are exempt but controlled in some states.

## What the pills feel like

Fioricet capsules are blue; generic tablets vary.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Butalbital's character is a mellow, drunk-like relaxation, with the caffeine adding a slight lift and the analgesic dulling headache pain.

Frequent use causes rebound headaches and dependence, and withdrawal can cause seizures.

With acetaminophen, liver damage is a hidden risk; see the [Fioricet product page](../products/fioricet.md).

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (15-30 min)

Butalbital is found mostly in tension-headache combinations (Fioricet and Esgic with acetaminophen and caffeine, Fiorinal with aspirin and caffeine, some with codeine). The onset is a gentle, warm relaxation that seems to start in the scalp, temples, neck and shoulders, exactly where tension headaches sit. The band of pressure around the head loosens and a light, drowsy heaviness spreads through the body.

Mentally there is a mild, calm ease and often a soft, slightly tipsy feeling. Because caffeine is in the mix, the effect can be oddly alert and talkative: relaxed and warm, but not necessarily sleepy, a combination some people find very appealing.

Some feel lightheaded, slightly nauseated or flushed. Coordination may already be a little off.

Medically, the onset reflects how quickly the barbiturate dissolves in fat and crosses into the brain. Butalbital has a half-life of about 35 hours and is processed by the liver. Short-acting barbiturates enter the brain fast; long-acting phenobarbital is slower.

Early signs are like alcohol intoxication: loosened inhibitions, slurred speech, unsteady walking, and slowed thinking. Judgment fades early, which is part of why accidental overdose was so common in the 20th century.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

Calm and looseness.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At the peak the headache recedes, muscles loosen and a mellow, warm calm settles in, often described as a light alcohol buzz with a clear head. The body feels pleasantly heavy and comfortable.

Emotionally people feel content, sociable and unbothered. With more, the effect turns into slurring, clumsiness and sleepiness, and the caffeine can make the person feel wired and sedated at once.

Taking extra of the combination products also means taking more acetaminophen, which can severely damage the liver, or more aspirin, which can cause bleeding and aspirin poisoning. Barbiturates have a narrow gap between a sedating amount and a fatal one, and unlike benzodiazepines they can stop breathing on their own. Very deep sleep that the person cannot be roused from, slow or irregular breathing, cold clammy skin, or blue lips is an emergency; with alcohol or opioids the danger rises steeply. Naloxone does not reverse barbiturates.

Medically, the peak is when GABA-A channels are held open longest. Unlike benzodiazepines, barbiturates at higher levels can open these channels directly, so there is no ceiling: breathing, blood pressure, and body temperature can all fall, and coma can follow.

The gap between sedation and fatal poisoning is narrow, and alcohol greatly narrows it further. There is no antidote like naloxone; treatment is breathing support in hospital.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

The plateau is a steady, relaxed drowsiness lasting several hours. The body stays warm and loose, with the neck and scalp free of the clamp-like headache, and a pleasant heaviness in the shoulders. Many people sit quietly, watch television or nap; the caffeine keeps some awake but mellow.

Concentration dulls, reaction time slows, and the person may feel slightly detached. Thoughts feel smooth and unhurried, with a gentle detachment from stress, though concentration on detailed work is poor and speech can be a little slow.

Driving is unsafe during this stretch, and alcohol adds both sedation and liver risk. Combining it with opioids, as in the codeine versions, deepens sedation and breathing risk.

### Comedown (4-8 h)

As it fades, the headache can return, sometimes worse than before. Frequent use causes medication-overuse 'rebound' headaches, which can trap people in a cycle of daily use. Some notice a tense, jittery feeling as both the butalbital and the caffeine wear off together.

Mood may be irritable or anxious, and sleep can be poor because of the caffeine and the rebound. The pull to take another dose to stop the returning headache is how daily use often begins.

After regular use, barbiturate withdrawal is among the most dangerous of any drug: anxiety, tremor, sweating, delirium, high fever and grand mal seizures can occur, and it can be fatal. Stopping must be medically supervised.

As levels fall, the person may remain clumsy and confused for hours, and barbiturates disturb normal sleep stages, so people often wake unrefreshed.

Tolerance to the pleasant effects builds faster than tolerance to the breathing effects, so the danger stays while the high fades. Withdrawal after daily use is among the most dangerous of any drug, with seizures, delirium, and high fever; it needs medical care.

### After-effects (8-24 h)

Drowsiness.

### Next day

Next-day grogginess.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, barbiturates cause calm, loosened inhibitions, and a drunk-like feeling. At higher doses, slurred speech, staggering, confusion, and deep sleep follow. As effects keep rising, the person cannot be woken, breathing and blood pressure fall, and body temperature drops: barbiturates can be fatal on their own, and far more easily with alcohol.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

## Overdose note for Butalbital (Fioricet, Fiorinal)

Overdose causes deep sedation and slowed breathing, plus acetaminophen liver damage or aspirin poisoning depending on the product.
