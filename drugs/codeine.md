# Codeine

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Natural opiate (opium alkaloid); weak mu-opioid agonist. A prodrug: the liver enzyme CYP2D6 turns part of it into morphine.

**Brand and street names:** Tylenol with Codeine (Tylenol #3, #4), Fioricet/Fiorinal with Codeine, Tuzistra XR, cough syrups such as Robitussin AC, Phenergan with Codeine (promethazine-codeine), Cheratussin AC; Co-codamol, Solpadeine, Nurofen Plus (UK).

**Brand and combination product pages:** [Tylenol with Codeine #3 and #4](../products/tylenol-3-4.md), [Fioricet with Codeine](../products/fioricet-codeine.md), [Fiorinal with Codeine](../products/fiorinal-codeine.md), [Tuzistra XR and other codeine cough products](../products/tuxarin.md).

## History and origins

French chemist Pierre-Jean Robiquet isolated codeine from opium in 1832. Its name comes from the Greek word for poppy head. For most of the 20th century it was the standard cough suppressant and a common mild painkiller, sold over the counter in many countries.

## Medical use, then and now

Mild to moderate pain (usually combined with acetaminophen), cough, and diarrhea. Since 2017 the FDA has banned it for children under 12 because some people, called ultra-rapid metabolizers, turn it into morphine so fast that children died.

## How it entered recreational use

Codeine cough syrup mixed with soda, called "lean", "purple drank", or "sizzurp", came out of the Houston hip-hop scene in the 1990s (DJ Screw's slowed-down "chopped and screwed" music) and spread nationwide. Several artists died from it or were hospitalized. Over-the-counter codeine misuse was also a large problem in the UK, Australia, and Nigeria, leading to tighter rules.

## Legal status

US: Schedule II alone; Schedule III in combination tablets; Schedule V in low-strength cough syrups. UK Class B. Available behind the pharmacy counter in some countries.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Codeine is mostly immediate-release. Extended-release codeine (Codeine Contin) is sold in Canada, and Tuzistra XR is an extended-release cough suspension in the US.

## What the pills feel like

Acetaminophen-codeine tablets (Tylenol #3 and generics) are usually round, white, fairly large tablets with a debossed imprint. Codeine cough syrups are liquids.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Codeine is the mildest of the commonly used opioids. People describe a soft, gentle, sleepy calm, with a pleasant body heaviness and a slight floaty feeling, rather than the strong euphoria of oxycodone or heroin. Itchiness is very common and often the most noticeable physical effect.

Because the effect depends on the CYP2D6 enzyme, people vary enormously: some feel almost nothing, others feel strong effects or become dangerously sedated. Promethazine in cough syrup adds heavy sedation and a sluggish, slowed-down, "syrupy" quality that people associate with lean.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (15-45 min)

Codeine by mouth comes on slowly and mildly. The first signs are a faint warmth in the stomach, a slight looseness in the shoulders, and often an early itch, typically on the face, scalp or upper back, because codeine releases histamine readily. Many people feel a light queasiness before anything pleasant.

Mentally the onset is a soft easing of tension: a dimming of anxiety and a slightly dreamy, sleepy calm rather than a rush. Thought slows a little, and people describe the edges of the day as rounded off.

How much a person feels varies enormously, because codeine itself is weak and must be converted to morphine by the liver enzyme CYP2D6. 'Poor metabolizers' feel little besides nausea and itch; 'ultra-rapid metabolizers' turn it into morphine fast and can feel strong effects and stop breathing. This is why codeine is no longer given to children.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Codeine is converted by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 into morphine, which produces most of its effect; genetics determine how fast this happens. Half-life about 3 hours. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The come-up is gentle and sleepy. People describe a mellow, floaty feeling and mild contentment, like the first glass of wine but heavier in the body. Nausea and dizziness are common. Whether anything more happens depends heavily on genetics: some people turn little codeine into morphine and feel mostly drowsiness and itch.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At the peak, warmth spreads through the body with a mild heaviness. Itch is often the most prominent body sensation, prickly and wandering. Nausea and a full, slow stomach are common, and the pupils narrow.

The emotional tone is gentle, drowsy contentment, a 'cozy' and somewhat dreamy state rather than euphoria. Many describe it as a weaker, sleepier, itchier relative of morphine, with a muffled sense of the world and slowed time.

Breathing is most depressed at the peak. Ultra-rapid metabolizers, people mixing codeine with alcohol or sedatives, and anyone taking combination products in large quantity (which adds acetaminophen, aspirin or ibuprofen toxicity) face the greatest danger.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

The plateau is calm and drowsy. Limbs feel pleasantly weighted, eyelids heavy, and light nodding can occur. Itching and flushing come and go, and the mouth stays dry.

Mentally people drift: daydreams, a passive comfortable mood, and reduced interest in effort. The pleasant effect is modest and often accompanied by sleepiness.

Constipation is particularly prominent with codeine, and the stomach can feel bloated. Dizziness on standing is common.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

The comedown is gradual and usually mild. Warmth fades, leaving tiredness, heaviness, and sometimes a headache.

Emotionally a slightly flat, sluggish mood follows; in regular users, restlessness and craving appear.

Constipation, dry mouth and nausea can linger. Daily users develop withdrawal like other opioids: yawning, runny nose, aches and anxiety.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (6-24 h)

Constipation, dry mouth, and a mild headache are common. Combination tablets with acetaminophen add liver strain that is not felt but is real.

### Next day

Most people feel close to normal the next day, maybe slightly foggy. After regular daily use, withdrawal is milder than with stronger opioids but real: restlessness, low mood, aches, runny nose, poor sleep, and diarrhea.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/codeine.md): Tablets and syrups; nearly all codeine use is oral.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/codeine.md): Codeine injection existed historically and in some countries (IM/SC); rare today.
- [Rectal (suppositories)](../routes/rectal/codeine.md): Codeine suppositories exist in some countries.

Not covered: No intranasal, transdermal, sublingual, or smoked medical forms; these are not typical routes.

## Overdose note for Codeine

Ultra-rapid metabolizers can overdose on ordinary amounts. Combination tablets carry a serious risk of liver failure from acetaminophen. Promethazine-codeine syrup combines two sedatives, increasing breathing suppression, particularly with alcohol or benzos.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
