# Solvents and gases (glue, gasoline, lighter fluid, paint thinner)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Volatile hydrocarbons that depress the brain like anesthetics.

**Brand and street names:** Huffing, sniffing, bagging; toluene, butane, propane, gasoline.

## History and origins

Solvent sniffing was reported in the 1950s and spread among young people, especially in poor communities and among Indigenous youth in Australia and Canada, because products are cheap and legal.

## Medical use, then and now

None.

## How it entered recreational use

Most common in early adolescence; inhalants are among the first substances some children try (NIDA Monitoring the Future).

## Legal status

Products are legal, but many US states restrict sales to minors and outlaw misuse.

## What the products look and feel like

Ordinary household products: glues, markers, fuels, correction fluid, lighter refills.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Solvents cause fast, alcohol-like intoxication: dizziness, giddiness, slurred speech and loss of inhibition, sometimes hallucinations.

Butane and other gases can trigger 'sudden sniffing death' from heart rhythm failure, even on the first use; suffocation and choking also kill.

Long-term toluene use damages the brain's white matter, causing memory and movement problems, and harms hearing, liver and kidneys.

Tolerance can develop with regular use, but the danger of sudden death does not decrease.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (seconds)

Volatile solvents, such as glue, paint thinner, petrol (gasoline), correction fluid and nail-polish remover, give off fumes that reach the brain within seconds of being breathed in. The first sensations are a rush of light-headedness, a buzzing or ringing in the ears, a numb, tingly face and hands, and a strong chemical smell and taste that can sting the nose, eyes and throat.

Mentally the onset feels like being suddenly and heavily drunk: giddy, silly, disinhibited and confused, with the world seeming to spin or shimmer. Some feel a brief euphoria or dreamlike detachment, and sounds may echo or distort.

Solvents can trigger 'sudden sniffing death', where the heart goes into a fatal rhythm, even on the first use, especially if the person is startled or runs. Inhaling with a bag over the head can cause suffocation, and the fumes are often flammable.

Solvents are highly fat-soluble and enter the brain within seconds, acting on GABA-A, NMDA and glycine receptors like anesthetics.

Early signs include dizziness, slurred speech and a chemical odor on the breath.

### Come-up (seconds-minutes)

Dizziness and giddiness rise quickly.

### Peak (1-5 min)

At the peak, which lasts only minutes, the body is clumsy and unsteady, speech slurs, vision may double or blur, and nausea or vomiting can occur. Muscles feel weak and the head heavy, and some people collapse.

Mentally the peak is confused and dreamlike, sometimes with hallucinations: patterns, distorted shapes, voices or a feeling of floating. Judgment is badly impaired, and some become aggressive or reckless. Time may seem to jump, and people often cannot say afterward how long they were out of it.

The biggest dangers are heart rhythm problems, choking on vomit while dazed, suffocation, burns and accidents. Startling or chasing someone who is intoxicated can raise the risk of cardiac arrest, so calm, gentle care matters.

At peak, solvents sensitize the heart muscle to adrenaline, so a surge from fright or exertion can trigger ventricular fibrillation (sudden sniffing death).

Hypoxia from bags, choking on vomit, and burns from flammable vapors are other major causes of death.

### Plateau (minutes)

Because the effect is so brief, people often keep inhaling to stay intoxicated, sometimes for hours. The body becomes increasingly drowsy, nauseated, headachy and uncoordinated, and a rash may form around the nose and mouth. Eyes may water and redden, and the person may cough and sneeze.

Mentally repeated inhaling keeps a person in a confused, numbed state, with memory gaps and swings between giddiness and irritation. Some drift into stupor.

Long-term use can damage the brain, nerves, liver, kidneys and heart, causing problems with memory, thinking, coordination, walking and hearing. Petrol containing lead and solvents like toluene are especially harmful.

### Comedown (15-45 min)

As the fumes clear, people often feel drowsy, dizzy and sick, with a pounding headache that can last hours. The nose and throat may be sore and the breath smells of chemicals. Appetite is often poor, and some feel shaky or weak for the rest of the day.

Mentally there may be confusion, poor concentration, irritability and low mood. Some barely remember what happened. Shame or secrecy can make it hard to talk about.

Regular use can lead to dependence, with craving, irritability and poor sleep on stopping. Support from youth and community services helps, and in many communities solvent use is linked to poverty and trauma. Some areas use low-aromatic fuel to reduce petrol sniffing.

Effects fade within minutes to an hour as vapors are exhaled.

Chronic toluene exposure causes white matter damage (toluene leukoencephalopathy), hearing loss, kidney acid-base problems, and liver injury; leaded gasoline adds lead poisoning.

### After-effects (hours)

Headache, nausea and drowsiness.

### Next day

Hangover; long-term damage with repeated use.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower exposures inhalants cause brief dizziness, giggling and lightheadedness. At higher exposures, confusion, hallucinations, loss of coordination and unconsciousness occur, and the risk of suffocation and sudden heart rhythm death rises sharply.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Solvents and gases (glue, gasoline, lighter fluid, paint thinner)

Sudden sniffing death, suffocation, burns and choking are the main dangers; there is no safe way to inhale solvents.

Read the shared page: [Inhalant safety: sudden sniffing death, poppers, nitrous oxide](../safety/inhalant-safety.md).
