# Crack cocaine

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Smokable base form of cocaine.

**Brand and street names:** Crack, rock, hard, base.

## History and origins

Crack spread in US cities in the mid-1980s; the 1986 Anti-Drug Abuse Act set far harsher penalties than for powder, a disparity reduced by the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010.

## Medical use, then and now

None.

## How it entered recreational use

Cheap and intensely reinforcing; associated with the 1980s crack epidemic.

## Legal status

Same as cocaine (US Schedule II).

## What the pills feel like

Crack looks like off-white rocks or chunks. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Crack produces an immediate, intense rush within seconds that fades within minutes, driving rapid, repeated use.

Craving is intense, and binges can last until the drug or money runs out.

Smoking causes lung injury ('crack lung'), burns and cracked lips; shared pipes can spread hepatitis C.

Tolerance to the euphoria builds quickly, while the heart and blood-pressure effects remain. Regular users often chase the first high and use more often.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (seconds)

Crack is cocaine in a smokable base form, and smoking delivers it to the brain within seconds. The body reacts almost instantly: the heart pounds hard and fast, the chest tightens, skin flushes or goes clammy, pupils widen and a ringing or rushing sound may fill the ears.

Mentally the onset is an explosive rush of intense euphoria, confidence and power, often described as the most intense pleasure a person has ever felt, compressed into a minute or two. Every thought feels urgent and brilliant, and the craving to repeat it starts almost immediately.

That speed is what makes crack so much more compulsive than snorted powder. Chest pain, a sense of the heart racing out of control or sudden severe headache in this window can signal heart attack or stroke and need emergency help.

Smoked cocaine reaches the brain in seconds, faster than snorting, producing a sharp dopamine spike.

Blood pressure and heart rate surge immediately.

### Come-up (seconds)

An intense rush.

### Peak (1-5 min)

The peak is brief, usually only several minutes. The body is wired and tense: jaw clenched, muscles twitchy, breathing quick, heart rate and blood pressure high, appetite gone and the mouth bone-dry.

Mentally the high is sharp, electric and self-assured, but it already carries an edge of watchfulness. Many people grow hyper-alert to sounds and movement, check windows and doors, or feel that others are watching them, an early taste of stimulant paranoia.

Because the peak is so short, people typically use again and again in binges. Each round raises heart strain and body temperature, and mixing with alcohol forms cocaethylene, which is more toxic to the heart.

At peak, the same heart and stroke risks as cocaine apply, plus lung injury (crack lung) from hot smoke.

Binge use can cause seizures and severe agitation.

### Plateau (5-15 min)

There is barely a true plateau. Within ten to twenty minutes the body is left jittery and depleted: sweating, restless legs, shaky hands, a racing pulse that does not match the fading pleasure, and sometimes a burning throat or cough from the smoke.

Mentally what remains is a buzzing anxiety and a fierce, consuming craving for more. Thinking narrows to getting the next hit; time, money, food, sleep and other people fall out of view. With continued use, paranoia, hearing things and suspicious searching ('ghost-chasing') can take over.

Binges can last hours or days. Overheating, seizures, irregular heartbeat and lung injury ('crack lung') are the main dangers, and street supplies may contain fentanyl, so naloxone and not using alone matter.

### Comedown (15-30 min)

The crash is fast and heavy. The body feels drained, achy and shaky, the heart may still flutter, and extreme exhaustion sets in, often followed by long sleep and ravenous hunger once the drug clears.

Emotionally the comedown is notoriously bleak: a hollow, irritable, anxious despair, sometimes with guilt and hopelessness or thoughts of suicide. Pleasure in ordinary things can feel absent for days.

Repeated use causes strong psychological dependence with intense cravings triggered by cues. There is no approved medicine for cocaine dependence yet, but counseling approaches such as contingency management work, and help lines can connect people to treatment.

The rapid fall in dopamine drives intense craving within minutes.

Chronic use damages lungs, causes burns to lips and fingers, and spreads hepatitis C through shared pipes.

### After-effects (hours)

Exhaustion after binges.

### Next day (days)

Days of low mood and craving.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses stimulants increase alertness, focus, energy and confidence and reduce appetite. At higher doses euphoria, talkativeness, restlessness, jaw clenching, sweating, a pounding heart and anxiety increase. At very high levels, paranoia, hallucinations, overheating, chest pain, seizures, heart attack and stroke can occur.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Crack cocaine

Overdose risks are the same as cocaine, made more likely by binging; fentanyl contamination is common.
