# Nitrous oxide (laughing gas)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Inhaled anesthetic gas (NMDA antagonist).

**Brand and street names:** Laughing gas, whippets, nos, hippy crack, balloons; medical: Entonox (mixed with oxygen).

## History and origins

Discovered by Joseph Priestley in 1772; Humphry Davy described its euphoria in 1799, and 'laughing gas parties' followed. Horace Wells used it for dental anesthesia in 1844. Recreational use surged in the 2010s, including large flavored tanks sold in smoke shops.

## Medical use, then and now

Dental and labor pain relief and light sedation, always mixed with oxygen.

## How it entered recreational use

Inhaled from balloons at festivals and parties.

## Legal status

Not federally scheduled in the US; selling it for inhaling is illegal in many states, and the FDA warned against flavored products in 2025. UK Class C since 2023.

## What the products look and feel like

Sold as small metal chargers or large colorful tanks; the released gas is extremely cold and can cause frostbite of lips and throat.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Nitrous gives a fast, short rush: tingling, a ringing or 'wah-wah' sound, laughter and dreamy detachment lasting a minute or two.

Dizziness and loss of balance cause falls. Breathing it without oxygen can cause loss of consciousness and suffocation.

Frequent or heavy use inactivates vitamin B12, which can cause numbness, weakness and difficulty walking from spinal cord damage, sometimes permanent.

Tolerance to dissociatives builds quickly, and ketamine tolerance leads people to use more, raising the risk of bladder and memory damage.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (seconds)

Nitrous oxide ('laughing gas', 'whippets', 'nos') is a gas that acts within seconds of being breathed in. The first sensation is a rising, buzzing tingle in the lips, fingers and toes, a warm heaviness in the limbs and a rhythmic, pulsing 'wah-wah' sound as hearing distorts and echoes.

Mentally the onset is giddy and dreamlike: laughter bubbles up easily, the world feels distant and funny, and thoughts begin to drift and loop. Some feel a sudden sense of profound insight that evaporates moments later, a hallmark of the gas.

Breathing it straight from a pressurized canister or tank can freeze the lips and throat and damage the lungs, and breathing it with no fresh air can starve the brain of oxygen. Standing up while inhaling leads to falls and head injuries when the legs give way.

Nitrous oxide is poorly soluble in blood, so it reaches equilibrium in the brain within seconds and leaves just as fast.

Breathing it without oxygen lowers blood oxygen; large tanks deliver very cold gas that can freeze tissue.

### Come-up (10-20 s)

A rush of tingling and sound distortion follows.

### Peak (20-60 s)

At its peak, which lasts only a minute or so, the body can feel weightless or very heavy, sound becomes a deep pulsing drone, vision may blur or tunnel, and coordination is lost. Some people go briefly numb and slump.

Mentally the peak is a short dissociative dream: the sense of self loosens, the room seems far away, and some people experience vivid fleeting visions or a feeling of touching a great truth. Laughter, confusion and a sense of being unable to finish a thought are common.

Fainting, falls, vomiting while dazed and oxygen deprivation are the main immediate dangers. Using it in cars, near water or with balloons over the face has led to deaths.

At the peak, NMDA blockade and endorphin release give dissociation and pain relief; falls and fainting are the main acute risks.

Using bags, masks, or enclosed spaces has caused deaths by asphyxiation.

### Plateau (1-2 min)

Nitrous oxide has almost no plateau unless it is inhaled repeatedly. With repeated breaths, the body stays tingly, numb and unsteady, the lips may feel cold, and dizziness and headache build. Repeated use keeps oxygen lower and raises the risk of fainting.

Mentally repeated breaths keep a person in a looping, giggly, disconnected state. Some feel the novelty fade and chase the first rush, taking more in quick succession, which mostly adds confusion rather than pleasure.

Heavy repeated use inactivates vitamin B12, and over weeks this can damage the spinal cord and nerves, causing numbness, tingling, weak legs and difficulty walking. Hospitals in several countries have reported rising numbers of young people with this nerve damage.

### Comedown (2-5 min)

The effect clears within a few minutes, leaving a light-headed, slightly dazed feeling, sometimes a headache or nausea. After a long session, people may feel tired, cold and wobbly. Some notice a metallic taste.

Mentally there is a quick return to normal, sometimes with a vague sense of having forgotten something important or embarrassment about laughing. Insights during the peak rarely survive.

Anyone who notices tingling, numbness, balance problems or weakness after regular use should stop and see a doctor, because B12-related nerve damage can be treated if caught early. Mixing nitrous with alcohol or other depressants adds risk of passing out and vomiting.

Effects end within minutes as the gas is breathed out.

Nitrous oxide irreversibly oxidizes the cobalt in vitamin B12, blocking the enzyme methionine synthase; repeated use causes spinal cord degeneration (myeloneuropathy), numbness and weakness, which needs B12 treatment and may not fully recover.

### After-effects (minutes)

Brief headache or dizziness.

### Next day (none (repeated use: nerve damage))

Normally none, but repeated heavy use causes nerve damage.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses dissociatives bring numbness, lightness, mild euphoria and dreamy detachment. At higher doses, the body feels far away, movement becomes difficult, and vision and sound distort. At still higher levels, people lose contact with surroundings entirely, cannot move or respond, and are at risk of injury, vomiting and choking, with breathing danger when combined with depressants.

More repeated inhalations raise the risk of fainting, falling and lack of oxygen, and, over weeks, nerve damage.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Nitrous oxide (laughing gas)

Deaths come from suffocation (especially with bags or masks), falls and accidents; heavy use causes B12-related nerve damage.
