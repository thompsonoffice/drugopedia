# Morphine

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Natural opiate (alkaloid of the opium poppy); full mu-opioid agonist. The reference drug every other opioid is compared to.

**Brand and street names:** MS Contin, Kadian, Avinza (discontinued), Roxanol, Duramorph, Infumorph, Oramorph (UK), Sevredol (UK), Embeda (with naltrexone, discontinued), Arymo ER (discontinued).

## History and origins

German pharmacist Friedrich Sertürner isolated morphine from opium around 1804 to 1817, the first alkaloid ever extracted from a plant. He named it after Morpheus, the Greek god of dreams. The invention of the hypodermic syringe in the 1850s made injected morphine common, and it was used heavily for wounded soldiers in the American Civil War and the Franco-Prussian War; dependence afterward was so common it was called "the soldier's disease". Morphine was sold freely in patent medicines until the Harrison Narcotics Act of 1914 in the US.

## Medical use, then and now

Still the gold standard for severe pain: after surgery, in cancer, in heart attacks, and in end-of-life care, where it also eases breathlessness. Given by mouth, by injection, by pump, into the spine, and as suppositories.

## How it entered recreational use

Morphine was one of the first widely misused drugs of the industrial age, especially among doctors, nurses, and wealthy patients in the 1800s. Today it is less popular recreationally than oxycodone or heroin because swallowed morphine is absorbed poorly, but diverted tablets and injection remain a factor.

## Legal status

US Schedule II. UK Class A (low-strength preparations lower). Controlled internationally under the 1961 Single Convention.

## What the pills feel like

MS Contin and generic extended-release morphine are small, round, film-coated tablets that feel smooth and hard. Immediate-release morphine tablets are often scored. Kadian is a capsule containing small pellets. Morphine oral solution is a liquid.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Morphine's euphoria is often called the most "classic" or "body-heavy" of the opioids. People describe it as warm, sleepy, and dreamy, more sedating and less energetic than oxycodone. It produces strong histamine release, so itching and flushing are especially common; many people describe scratching their nose or face constantly.

Compared with heroin (which turns into morphine in the body), morphine taken the same way is described as slower to arrive and less sharp, with a smaller rush and more nausea. Compared with hydromorphone or fentanyl it is mellower and longer. Many people report a pleasant, hazy, dream-laden nod rather than a clear-headed buzz.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (15-45 min)

Swallowed morphine announces itself slowly and often through the stomach first: a vague queasiness or heaviness, then warmth spreading from the belly. Many people notice the itch before any pleasure, typically a prickle at the tip of the nose, the scalp, or the chest.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Morphine is processed mainly by glucuronidation in the liver into morphine-3-glucuronide and the active morphine-6-glucuronide, both cleared by the kidneys; its half-life is about 2 to 4 hours. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

Morphine's come-up is a slow tide rather than a wave. Limbs feel weighted and warm, the face flushes, and a drowsy, dreamy contentment builds. Nausea peaks here for many, and some vomit, which people new to opioids often find surprising given how relaxed they otherwise feel.

### Peak (1-2 h)

The peak is the classic morphine state: heavy, warm, sleepy bliss, with the body feeling sunk into the bed or chair. Thinking becomes slow and soft, and pain, even severe pain, stops seeming to matter. Pupils shrink, breathing slows, and heavy itching is common. This is when breathing is most depressed.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

Morphine's plateau is dominated by the nod: drifting into dreamy, half-awake imagery, sometimes vivid and story-like, then surfacing again. People describe losing track of time and conversations. Urinating can be difficult, and the mouth dries out.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

As morphine fades, the warmth recedes unevenly: the dreaminess goes first, the heaviness and itch linger. People describe a sleepy, foggy flatness, and many simply fall asleep. With immediate-release morphine this happens 3 to 6 hours in; extended-release stretches it far longer.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (6-24 h)

Constipation is the most persistent after-effect, along with dry mouth and a dull headache. Morphine's metabolite morphine-6-glucuronide is active and builds up if the kidneys are weak, so in some people sedation outlasts the high by many hours.

### Next day

The next day often brings grogginess and a 'hangover' of lethargy. After repeated use, withdrawal begins roughly 8 to 24 hours after the last exposure: yawning, runny nose, watery eyes, aches, chills, diarrhea, and restlessness, with a flu-like peak at 2 to 3 days.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/morphine.md): Tablets, capsules, oral solution; immediate- and extended-release.
- [Rectal (suppositories)](../routes/rectal/morphine.md): Suppositories, mainly in palliative care.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/morphine.md): IV, IM, SC, and spinal (epidural/intrathecal) in hospitals; also misused by injection.
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/morphine.md): Not a common medical route; occasionally misused; absorbed poorly compared with other routes.

Not covered: Not available as transdermal patch, sublingual product, or smoked form in medicine. Smoking opium (which contains morphine) is covered under Opium.

## Overdose note for Morphine

Morphine and its metabolites build up in people with kidney disease and can cause delayed, prolonged overdose. Extended-release morphine taken in a damaged form releases a full day's supply at once.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
