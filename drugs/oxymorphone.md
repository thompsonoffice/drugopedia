# Oxymorphone

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic opioid; a strong full mu-opioid agonist, and also a metabolite of oxycodone.

**Brand and street names:** Opana, Opana ER (removed from the US market in 2017), Numorphan (older injection and suppository).

## History and origins

Developed in Germany in 1914 and patented in the US in 1955; Endo introduced Numorphan in 1959. Opana ER came in 2006. In 2017 the FDA asked Endo to withdraw reformulated Opana ER, the first time it removed an opioid over misuse, after injection of the reformulated tablets caused an HIV and hepatitis C outbreak in Scott County, Indiana, and a blood disorder called thrombotic microangiopathy.

## Medical use, then and now

Moderate to severe pain. Immediate-release generic tablets remain available in the US.

## How it entered recreational use

Numorphan was popular among people who injected drugs in the 1960s and 1970s ("blues"). Opana ER was heavily misused in the 2010s.

## Legal status

US Schedule II.

## What it feels like

### The character of its euphoria

Oxymorphone is described as strong, fast, and very heavy: more intensely euphoric and more sedating than oxycodone, with a powerful, sleepy body high. People often compare it to heroin more than to other pills.

It is absorbed poorly when swallowed, so experiences by mouth vary a lot. Many describe a deep, warm, sinking feeling and long nodding.

Tolerance changes everything below. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (15-45 min)

The first sign is often subtle: a faint warmth in the stomach or chest, a slight heaviness behind the eyes, and a loosening of tension in the shoulders. People describe it as the edge coming off whatever they were feeling. With Oxymorphone, many say they notice their breathing slow slightly and their thoughts get quieter before they feel anything they would call pleasure.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The warmth spreads outward to the arms, legs, and face. Pain, if present, fades into the background rather than disappearing outright; people describe still knowing it is there but no longer caring. A calm, contented mood rises. Itching, usually moderate, often starts on the nose, face, or upper body, caused by histamine release and by opioid effects on the nervous system. Some people feel a wave of nausea at this point, especially if they are new to opioids or moving around.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At the peak, people describe a deep, cozy contentment, as if wrapped in a warm blanket, with worries feeling far away and unimportant. Body feels heavy and relaxed; speech can slow. Pupils shrink to pinpoints. Described as one of the most intense pill highs, close to heroin. This is also when breathing is most suppressed and overdose is most likely, often without the person noticing anything wrong.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

The effects level off into a steady, calm state. "Nodding" (heavy with Oxymorphone) can occur: drifting in and out of a light sleep, head dropping, then jerking awake, sometimes mid-sentence or with a cigarette or drink in hand. People report vivid, dreamlike half-sleep. Talkativeness or a quiet, inward contentment are both common. Mouth dryness, flushed skin, and difficulty urinating can appear.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

The glow fades gradually. People describe the world getting "louder" again: light, noise, and small problems regain their weight. Tiredness and a flat, slightly gray mood are common. Pain, if it was being treated, returns. In people with dependence, this is where restlessness, yawning, and craving start.

### After-effects (6-24 h)

Over the following hours: constipation, dry mouth, mild headache, and sluggishness. Sleep may be heavy but not restful. Some feel a mild low mood or irritability as the drug clears.

### Next day

After a single medical exposure, most people feel close to normal, perhaps a little foggy or constipated. After repeated use, the next day can bring early withdrawal: anxiety, runny nose, goosebumps, aches, diarrhea, and strong craving. Tolerance builds quickly, which means the same effect needs more drug and overdose risk rises, especially after a break, when tolerance drops but habits do not.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/oxymorphone.md): Immediate- and extended-release tablets; absorption is poor and food increases it.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/oxymorphone.md): Medical injection (Numorphan, historical/hospital); misuse of tablets by injection caused the Indiana outbreak.
- [Rectal (suppositories)](../routes/rectal/oxymorphone.md): Numorphan suppositories (discontinued).
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/oxymorphone.md): Misused by snorting; absorption through the nose is much higher than swallowing, raising overdose risk.

Not covered: No transdermal, sublingual, or smoked forms.

## Overdose note for Oxymorphone

Taking it with food or alcohol can sharply raise blood levels. The removed Opana ER caused a deadly blood disorder and an HIV outbreak when injected.

Read the shared page: [What an overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md).
