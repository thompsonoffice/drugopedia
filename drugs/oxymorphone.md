# Oxymorphone

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic opioid; a strong full mu-opioid agonist, and also a metabolite of oxycodone.

**Brand and street names:** Opana, Opana ER (removed from the US market in 2017), Numorphan (older injection and suppository).

## History and origins

Developed in Germany in 1914 and patented in the US in 1955; Endo introduced Numorphan in 1959. Opana ER came in 2006. In 2017 the FDA asked Endo to withdraw reformulated Opana ER, the first time it removed an opioid over misuse, after injection of the reformulated tablets caused an HIV and hepatitis C outbreak in Scott County, Indiana, and a blood disorder called thrombotic microangiopathy.

## Medical use, then and now

Moderate to severe pain. Immediate-release generic tablets remain available in the US.

## How it entered recreational use

Numorphan was popular among people who injected drugs in the 1960s and 1970s ("blues"). Opana ER was heavily misused in the 2010s.

## Legal status

US Schedule II.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Immediate-release oxymorphone (Opana and generics) lasts about 4 to 6 hours. Extended-release oxymorphone (Opana ER) was given every 12 hours and had a slower, longer, steadier profile. Reformulated Opana ER was removed from the US market in 2017, though some generic ER oxymorphone remained for a time. The withdrawn reformulation is linked to the 2015 HIV and hepatitis C outbreak in Indiana.

**Extra risks of extended-release:** Extended-release products hold far more drug than a single immediate-release tablet, released slowly over many hours. If the release system is damaged, chewed, crushed, dissolved, or taken with alcohol (for some products), the whole reserve can be released at once, which has caused many overdose deaths. ER products are meant only for people with established opioid tolerance and ongoing pain, and they should never be split or shared. Because they last so long, overdose can develop slowly, often during sleep.

## What the pills feel like

Forms vary by country and manufacturer; see the brand list above. Ask a pharmacist to describe the product you have.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Oxymorphone is described as strong, fast, and very heavy: more intensely euphoric and more sedating than oxycodone, with a powerful, sleepy body high. People often compare it to heroin more than to other pills.

It is absorbed poorly when swallowed, so experiences by mouth vary a lot. Many describe a deep, warm, sinking feeling and long nodding.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (20-45 min)

Oxymorphone (Opana) taken by mouth is absorbed poorly and slowly at first, so the onset is gradual: a growing warmth in the chest and a slow loosening of the muscles.

Mentally the onset is calm, steady relief that builds rather than rushes.

Pupils narrow, the mouth dries, and some itch and nausea can appear. Taking it with food or alcohol can sharply increase how much is absorbed.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Oxymorphone is glucuronidated in the liver; its oral bioavailability is only about 10 percent and food raises it. Half-life about 7 to 9 hours. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (45-90 min)

The come-up is heavy and warm: a strong pull toward the couch, flushing, and a powerful wave of comfort. Itch is moderate.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At the peak the body feels very heavy and warm, with deep muscle relaxation and slowed breathing.

The emotional tone is strong, sedated euphoria; people describe it as among the most potent pill opioids, with heavy, dreamy contentment.

Oxymorphone is highly potent. The reformulated Opana ER was withdrawn in 2017 after its injection misuse was linked to an HIV and hepatitis C outbreak and a blood-clotting disorder.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

Oxymorphone's plateau, especially with extended-release forms, is long and heavily sedated. The body feels sunk and slack, skin is warm and flushed, and nodding is frequent. Itch is usually mild.

Mentally the plateau is drowsy and dreamlike; people drift in and out of awareness, and conversation fades. The euphoria settles into deep, sleepy contentment, and time passes without notice.

Breathing remains suppressed for many hours, and the long duration means overdose can develop during sleep. Constipation and dry mouth build.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

The comedown is slow for extended-release tablets and quicker for immediate-release: heaviness lifts into tiredness and aching.

Emotionally people describe a flat, gray mood and restlessness as the strong calm drains away.

Regular use leads to dependence; withdrawal brings sweating, yawning, goosebumps, cramps and craving. Taking more during the comedown stacks on drug still in the body.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (6-24 h)

After-effects include constipation, sweating, and grogginess. People often describe feeling drained.

### Next day

After regular use, withdrawal is strong and comparable to heroin's: aches, restless legs, sweating, diarrhea, and insomnia.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/oxymorphone.md): Immediate- and extended-release tablets; absorption is poor and food increases it.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/oxymorphone.md): Medical injection (Numorphan, historical/hospital); misuse of tablets by injection caused the Indiana outbreak.
- [Rectal (suppositories)](../routes/rectal/oxymorphone.md): Numorphan suppositories (discontinued).
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/oxymorphone.md): Misused by snorting; absorption through the nose is much higher than swallowing, raising overdose risk.

Not covered: No transdermal, sublingual, or smoked forms.

## Overdose note for Oxymorphone

Taking it with food or alcohol can sharply raise blood levels. The removed Opana ER caused a deadly blood disorder and an HIV outbreak when injected.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
