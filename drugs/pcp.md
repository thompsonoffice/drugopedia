# PCP (phencyclidine)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Dissociative anesthetic (NMDA antagonist).

**Brand and street names:** Sernyl (former brand); street: angel dust, wet, sherm, fry, 'embalming fluid' cigarettes.

## History and origins

Developed by Parke-Davis in the 1950s as the anesthetic Sernyl and withdrawn from human use in 1965 because patients woke with severe agitation and delirium. Recreational use rose in the late 1960s and 1970s.

## Medical use, then and now

None in humans today; formerly a veterinary anesthetic.

## How it entered recreational use

PCP-dipped cigarettes remain in some US cities.

## Legal status

US Schedule II; UN 1971 Convention Schedule II.

## What the products look and feel like

Pure PCP is a white powder; street PCP is often a liquid with a chemical smell. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

PCP's character is numb, distorted detachment combined with stimulation: people may feel invulnerable while their sense of body and surroundings is warped.

It can cause agitation, paranoia, aggression and a psychosis resembling schizophrenia, and people often do not feel pain, so injuries are common.

High blood pressure, high body temperature, muscle breakdown and seizures can occur; effects can come and go in waves over a day or more.

Tolerance to dissociatives builds quickly, and ketamine tolerance leads people to use more, raising the risk of bladder and memory damage.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (2-5 min)

PCP (phencyclidine, 'angel dust') was developed as an anesthetic in the 1950s and dropped because patients woke agitated and delirious. Smoked, it comes on within minutes; swallowed, over a longer while. The body feels numb and distant, especially in the hands and feet, movements become stiff and clumsy, and eyes may flick rapidly side to side (nystagmus).

Mentally the onset is a growing sense of detachment: the body feels rubbery or not one's own, sounds become echoey and distant, and thoughts begin to fragment. Some feel euphoric or powerful; others feel confused and threatened.

PCP's effect is unpredictable even for experienced users, and its numbing means people can be badly hurt without feeling it.

PCP is fat-soluble and long-lasting, with a half-life that can be about 1 to 2 days, and it is stored in fat and recirculated.

It raises blood pressure, heart rate and temperature and causes characteristic jerky eye movements in several directions.

### Come-up (5-30 min)

Detachment and blunted pain build.

### Peak (15-60 min)

At its peak the body may be rigid or floppy, with high blood pressure, a fast heart, sweating, drooling and a blank stare. Pain is blocked, and people can show startling strength because they do not register strain.

Mentally the peak ranges from dreamy dissociation to frank psychosis: distorted body image, hallucinations, paranoia, grandiosity and loss of contact with reality. Some become withdrawn and catatonic; others become agitated, terrified or violent.

Dangers include severe injuries, drowning, seizures, very high body temperature and blood pressure, muscle breakdown that harms the kidneys, and coma. Emergency care is needed for agitation, overheating or unresponsiveness.

At the peak, NMDA blockade plus effects on dopamine and sigma receptors produce agitation, psychosis, catatonia, and insensitivity to pain.

Severe toxicity includes very high temperature, muscle breakdown, kidney failure, seizures and coma.

### Plateau (4-6 h)

The plateau is long and uneven, lasting hours, with waves of clarity and confusion. The body stays numb and uncoordinated, blood pressure high, and speech slurred or halting. Breathing may be irregular, and muscles can stay tense or jerky; a calm, dim room helps more than physical restraint.

Mentally people may drift between dreamlike calm and sudden fear or anger, often without remembering what triggered it. Time, place and identity can feel scrambled. Speaking can be difficult, and people may answer slowly, repeat themselves, or stare silently, unable to put thoughts into words.

Because PCP is stored in body fat, effects can resurface or linger for a day or more, and staying in a quiet, low-stimulation place with sober help reduces danger.

### Comedown (6-24 h)

The comedown is slow and blurry: fatigue, muscle soreness, headache and poor coordination may last into the next day. Blood pressure may stay high, and some people feel achy all over from muscle strain they did not notice at the time.

Emotionally people often feel confused, anxious, depressed or ashamed, with patchy memory; some experience lingering paranoia or psychosis for days. Mood can swing between flatness and agitation, and sleep is often broken by vivid or disturbing dreams.

Regular use can cause memory problems, speech difficulties, depression and lasting psychotic symptoms; mental-health care is important if symptoms persist. Family and friends may notice personality changes; support and follow-up are worth seeking even after one frightening episode.

Because PCP lingers and recirculates, symptoms can fluctuate for days.

Repeated use causes memory problems, depression and speech difficulty that can last months.

### After-effects (1-2 days)

Confusion, memory gaps, low mood.

### Next day (days)

Lingering effects can last days.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses dissociatives bring numbness, lightness, mild euphoria and dreamy detachment. At higher doses, the body feels far away, movement becomes difficult, and vision and sound distort. At still higher levels, people lose contact with surroundings entirely, cannot move or respond, and are at risk of injury, vomiting and choking, with breathing danger when combined with depressants.

Effects are unpredictable at any amount; higher levels bring catatonia, seizures and dangerous agitation.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for PCP (phencyclidine)

PCP poisoning causes agitation, very high temperature, seizures, coma and death, as well as deaths from injuries and drowning.

Read the shared page: [Dissociative safety: K-holes, injuries, bladder damage, mixing](../safety/dissociative-safety.md).
