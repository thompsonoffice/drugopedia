# Tramadol

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Synthetic, weak mu-opioid agonist (mostly through its metabolite O-desmethyltramadol, made by CYP2D6) that also blocks reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine, like an antidepressant.

**Brand and street names:** Ultram, Ultram ER, Ultracet (with acetaminophen), ConZip, Ryzolt (discontinued), Tramal, Zydol (UK), Seglentis (with celecoxib).

**Brand and combination product pages:** [Ultracet](../products/ultracet.md).

## History and origins

Developed by the German company Grünenthal in 1962 and marketed from 1977; approved in the US in 1995. It was long marketed as low-risk and was not federally controlled in the US until 2014.

## Medical use, then and now

Moderate pain.

## How it entered recreational use

Widely misused in West Africa, the Middle East, and North Africa, where cheap high-strength tablets ('tramol', 'Tamol') are trafficked, and among people seeking a milder opioid in the US and Europe.

## Legal status

US Schedule IV (since 2014). UK Class C.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Immediate-release tramadol (Ultram, generics, Ultracet with acetaminophen) starts within about 15 to 60 minutes and lasts about 6 hours. Extended-release tramadol (Ultram ER, ConZip, the discontinued Ryzolt) is taken once daily, with a slower rise and steadier, milder effect. ER tramadol still carries seizure and serotonin syndrome risk.

**Extra risks of extended-release:** Extended-release products hold far more drug than a single immediate-release tablet, released slowly over many hours. If the release system is damaged, chewed, crushed, dissolved, or taken with alcohol (for some products), the whole reserve can be released at once, which has caused many overdose deaths. ER products are meant only for people with established opioid tolerance and ongoing pain, and they should never be split or shared. Because they last so long, overdose can develop slowly, often during sleep.

## What the pills feel like

Per DailyMed labels, many generic immediate-release tramadol tablets are white, round, and film-coated, so they feel smooth; some strengths have a score line, others are plain on one side. Shapes differ between manufacturers (some are oblong or capsule-shaped). Extended-release tablets are unscored.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Tramadol is described as a mild, stimulating, 'happy' opioid: a mix of light opioid warmth and an antidepressant-like lift in mood and energy, sometimes with restlessness. Many say it feels more like a pleasant buzz than a nod.

Side effects are distinctive: nausea, sweating, dizziness, and feeling 'wired' or anxious. Withdrawal can include unusual symptoms like tingling, panic, and confusion, because of its serotonin and norepinephrine effects.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (15-60 min)

Tramadol by mouth starts gently: a slight warmth and a mild lift in mood, often with a hint of nausea and lightheadedness.

Mentally the onset can feel mildly energizing and antidepressant-like, because tramadol also increases serotonin and noradrenaline; some describe feeling chatty and upbeat rather than sleepy.

Dry mouth, sweating, dizziness and nausea are common early on.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Tramadol is turned by CYP2D6 into O-desmethyltramadol, which carries most of the opioid effect; the parent drug provides serotonin and norepinephrine effects. Half-life about 6 hours. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The come-up feels energetic and talkative for many, more like an antidepressant than an opioid. Some feel restless, sweaty, or nauseous.

### Peak (2-3 h)

At the peak people feel mild warmth, relaxation and reduced pain, sometimes with restlessness or a tingling, slightly 'wired' feeling.

The emotional tone is mild euphoria with an energized, talkative quality unusual for opioids.

Tramadol can cause seizures, especially at high levels or with other drugs, and serotonin syndrome when combined with antidepressants. Its effect depends on CYP2D6 conversion, so some people get almost nothing and others get strong opioid effects.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (2-6 h)

The plateau brings steady mild relief with warmth, sweating and sometimes itch.

Mentally people feel calm or slightly stimulated.

Nausea and dizziness may continue.

### Comedown (6-10 h)

The comedown brings tiredness and sometimes irritability.

Some notice low mood or anxiety.

Regular use causes dependence, and withdrawal can include both opioid symptoms and antidepressant-like symptoms such as anxiety, 'brain zaps' and confusion.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (10-24 h)

Sweating, dizziness, and constipation. Seizure risk is present throughout.

### Next day

Next day can bring a mood dip. Withdrawal includes typical opioid symptoms plus odd ones: tingling, panic, confusion, and sometimes hallucinations.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

At higher doses tramadol's seizure risk rises steeply, and serotonin toxicity (agitation, sweating, twitching, fever) may appear, in addition to opioid sedation and slowed breathing.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## Overdose note for Tramadol

Tramadol can cause seizures, even at ordinary amounts and more in overdose or with other drugs that lower the seizure threshold. Combined with antidepressants and other serotonergic drugs it can cause serotonin syndrome (agitation, high fever, muscle twitching, confusion). Naloxone reverses breathing suppression but not seizures.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
