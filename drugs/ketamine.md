# Ketamine

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Dissociative anesthetic (NMDA receptor antagonist).

**Brand and street names:** Ketalar; street: K, special K, ket, kitty.

## History and origins

Synthesized by Calvin Stevens at Parke-Davis in 1962 as a safer replacement for PCP; first tested in humans in 1964, FDA-approved in 1970, and used in the Vietnam War. Recreational use spread in the 1970s and in 1990s club scenes.

## Medical use, then and now

An anesthetic that largely preserves breathing, used in emergencies, children and veterinary medicine; now also used off-label in clinics for depression and pain.

## How it entered recreational use

Used in club and festival settings for dissociation and the 'K-hole'. Frequent use leads to bladder damage.

## Legal status

US Schedule III (1999). UK Class B. Not controlled under the UN conventions; the WHO has recommended against international control because of its medical importance.

## What the products look and feel like

Medical ketamine is a clear liquid in vials. Street ketamine is a white crystalline powder. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Ketamine's character is floating detachment: the body feels rubbery or distant, sounds echo, and the room seems to slide. Thought becomes dreamlike and slow.

At higher levels people enter the 'K-hole', near-total disconnection where the body seems to vanish; some report out-of-body or near-death experiences, and they cannot move or speak.

Nausea, unsteadiness and loss of pain sensation make falls and injuries common, and the bladder damage from regular use (ketamine uropathy) can be severe and permanent.

Tolerance to dissociatives builds quickly, and ketamine tolerance leads people to use more, raising the risk of bladder and memory damage.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (5-15 min)

Ketamine's onset varies with route, from seconds to minutes when snorted or injected to longer by mouth. People first notice a numb, floaty heaviness in the limbs, a tingling or buzzing in the hands and face, and a sense that their body is slightly delayed when they move.

Mentally the world starts to feel distant and unreal. Sounds can seem echoey or tinny, colors and edges shift, and thoughts slow and drift. Many describe a dreamy, detached euphoria.

Heart rate and blood pressure rise. Nausea is common, and walking becomes unsteady quickly, which is a major cause of injuries.

Snorted ketamine is absorbed through the nasal lining; intravenous ketamine reaches the brain within seconds, which is why anesthesia uses it.

Ketamine raises heart rate and blood pressure by releasing noradrenaline, unlike most anesthetics, and causes nystagmus and increased saliva.

### Come-up (10-20 min)

Within minutes the body starts to feel distant; walking becomes unsteady.

### Peak (15-30 min)

At the peak, the body can feel numb, weightless or disconnected; limbs feel rubbery or absent, and moving is difficult. Vision blurs, and the eyes may flick side to side (nystagmus). Pain sensation is greatly reduced.

The emotional tone ranges from dreamy and euphoric to confusing or frightening. At higher levels people may enter the 'K-hole': a deep dissociation in which awareness of the body and surroundings fades, and people may feel they are floating, traveling, or dying.

Risks include falls, drowning, choking on vomit, and being unable to respond to danger. Combining with alcohol, GHB, opioids or benzodiazepines can depress breathing. Street ketamine may be mixed with other drugs.

At the peak, NMDA blockade in the thalamus and cortex disconnects sensory input from awareness; protective airway reflexes are partly preserved, but vomiting while unresponsive can still cause choking.

Emergence reactions (vivid dreams, confusion, agitation) are known from anesthesia, and similar experiences occur in recreational use.

### Plateau (30-60 min)

The plateau keeps a heavy, numb, detached body. Movements are clumsy, speech is slow, and people often prefer to lie still.

Mentally people drift in a dreamy state with slowed or fragmented time. Thoughts can feel profound or nonsensical, and memory is patchy.

Coordination remains poor, so falls and accidents remain common.

### Comedown (1-2 h)

The comedown is relatively quick. Numbness fades, leaving heaviness, grogginess and sometimes nausea.

Emotionally some feel calm and reflective, an 'afterglow' linked to ketamine's antidepressant effects; others feel confused, flat or anxious.

Frequent use brings tolerance, cravings, memory problems and ketamine bladder syndrome, with painful, frequent urination and bladder damage.

Ketamine's half-life is about 2 to 3 hours; norketamine is active and extends after-effects.

Chronic use damages the bladder lining (ketamine cystitis), causing pain, urgency and blood in urine; stopping early can allow recovery, but severe cases need surgery. Liver and bile duct injury is also reported.

### After-effects (2-4 h)

Tiredness and a dreamy afterglow; some feel low.

### Next day

Next-day fatigue; with frequent use, bladder pain and urgency.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses dissociatives bring numbness, lightness, mild euphoria and dreamy detachment. At higher doses, the body feels far away, movement becomes difficult, and vision and sound distort. At still higher levels, people lose contact with surroundings entirely, cannot move or respond, and are at risk of injury, vomiting and choking, with breathing danger when combined with depressants.

The gap between 'floaty' and a K-hole is small, and mixing with depressants can stop breathing.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Ketamine

Ketamine alone rarely stops breathing, but with alcohol, opioids or benzodiazepines it can; vomiting while unresponsive and accidents are major causes of death.
