# Benztropine and trihexyphenidyl as misused

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Anticholinergic medicines for Parkinson's and drug-induced movement disorders.

**Brand and street names:** Cogentin (benztropine), Artane (trihexyphenidyl).

## History and origins

Trihexyphenidyl was approved in 1949 and benztropine in 1954. Misuse is reported among psychiatric patients and in prisons.

## Medical use, then and now

Parkinson's disease and side effects of antipsychotics.

## How it entered recreational use

Misused for euphoria and hallucinations.

## Legal status

Prescription-only.

## What the products look and feel like

Small white tablets.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

At high levels, these cause euphoria, then confusion and delirium.

Overheating, urinary retention and fast heart rate are common.

Misuse risk is higher in people already taking antipsychotics.

Tolerance develops to some effects, but delirium risk remains.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (about 1 h)

Many prescription medicines with anticholinergic effects, such as benztropine, trihexyphenidyl (used in Parkinson's and for antipsychotic side effects), oxybutynin and some older antihistamines, can be misused or cause poisoning. Effects build over an hour or so: dry mouth, blurred vision, flushed skin and a faster heart. Some feel restless and notice a lightness in the head.

Mentally some people feel a mild buzz or energy at lower amounts with trihexyphenidyl, but higher amounts cause confusion and dreamlike disorientation. Concentration drifts.

Misuse is seen among people prescribed these medicines alongside antipsychotics. Large amounts are dangerous.

Both are absorbed well.

Anticholinergic signs.

### Come-up (1-2 h)

Dry mouth.

### Peak (2-4 h)

At the peak of poisoning the body is hot, dry and flushed, with a racing heart, wide pupils and urinary retention. Muscles may twitch, speech may slur and walking may be unsteady. The skin feels hot because sweating stops, and urination is difficult. The heart may pound and the person may collapse. Pupils stay wide.

Mentally there can be delirium, hallucinations and agitation. People may see insects or shadow figures, talk to people who aren't there, and lose track of where they are. Agitation and fear are common. Memory is often lost.

Emergency care is needed for seizures, heart problems or overheating. Medical teams may use physostigmine under monitoring.

Delirium at high levels.

Overheating is a danger.

### Plateau (4-12 h)

The plateau lasts hours. The body stays dry and racing. Pupils stay wide, vision is blurry and light uncomfortable, the mouth and skin dry, and the heart keeps racing. Constipation and urinary retention may become painful. Breathing may quicken.

Mentally confusion persists. Confusion and hallucinations may continue, and the person may wander or act out tasks that aren't happening. Memory for this time is often lost. Some talk in mumbles and pick at the air or their clothing, and mood can swing between fear and blankness.

Older adults are especially vulnerable. Someone should stay with them and call poison control or emergency services. Hospital monitoring may be needed.

### Comedown (12-24 h)

The comedown brings tiredness, dry mouth and blurred vision. Blurred vision, dry mouth, constipation and exhaustion may linger for a day or more, and the person may feel weak and shaky. Muscles may ache from twitching.

Mentally confusion may linger. Memory gaps, anxiety and low mood are common, and some people feel frightened by what they remember. Sleep may be broken by vivid dreams. Anxiety may linger.

Doctors can adjust medicines to reduce misuse risk. People prescribed these medicines who notice cravings or misuse should talk to their doctor. Never stop a prescribed medicine suddenly without advice. A doctor can check the heart and bladder.

Long half-life for benztropine.

Confusion can linger.

### After-effects (1-2 days)

Confusion.

### Next day (days)

Next-day fog.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses deliriants cause drowsiness, dry mouth and a fast heartbeat. At higher doses, confusion, blurred vision and hallucinations start. At very high levels, full delirium with realistic hallucinations, overheating, seizures and dangerous heart rhythms occur; no one finds this pleasant.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: body size, tolerance, genetics, mood and setting, health conditions, medicines (especially antidepressants and lithium), and product purity all change the effect.
