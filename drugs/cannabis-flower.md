# Cannabis flower (marijuana)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Plant cannabis containing THC (the main intoxicant) and CBD (non-intoxicating).

**Brand and street names:** Marijuana, weed, pot, grass, bud, herb; strains marketed as 'indica' or 'sativa'.

## History and origins

Cannabis has been used for at least 2,500 years; residues were found in burial braziers in western China. It entered Western medicine in the 1840s through William O'Shaughnessy, was effectively banned by the 1937 Marihuana Tax Act, removed from the US Pharmacopeia in 1942, and placed in Schedule I in 1970. California legalized medical use in 1996; Colorado and Washington legalized adult use in 2012.

## Medical use, then and now

Historically a tincture for pain, spasms and sleep. Today, state medical programs use it for chronic pain, chemotherapy nausea, and spasticity; FDA-approved cannabinoid medicines are dronabinol, nabilone, and Epidiolex (CBD).

## How it entered recreational use

Recreational use spread through jazz culture in the 1930s, the 1960s counterculture, and today's legal markets. Average THC in seized flower rose from about 4 percent in the 1990s to around 15 percent or more today (NIDA).

## Legal status

Federally Schedule I in the US (a 2024 proposal to move it to Schedule III was still under review). Legal for adults in many US states and in Canada, Uruguay, Germany (limited) and elsewhere; controlled under the UN 1961 Single Convention.

## What the products look and feel like

Dried flower is sticky, crumbly plant matter with a strong skunky smell. Legal-market flower is labeled with THC content; illicit products can be sprayed with synthetic cannabinoids. Street and online products are frequently mislabeled; lab testing services have found products with different drugs or none of the advertised one.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Smoked or vaped flower brings a quick, wave-like high: relaxed body, laughter, brighter music and food, and wandering, associative thinking. Strains feel different mainly because of THC strength and the THC-to-CBD balance, more than the 'indica/sativa' label.

Short-term memory and time sense go first: a sentence started can be forgotten mid-way, and minutes stretch. Red eyes, dry mouth and the 'munchies' are classic.

With strong flower, the same high can tip into racing heart, paranoia and a feeling of being stuck in a loop, especially for new users or people prone to anxiety or psychosis.

Tolerance to the high builds quickly with daily use and fades after a break of a few weeks; people returning after a break, or switching to concentrates or edibles, are often surprised by how strong the effect is.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the inhaled and smoked route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (seconds to minutes)

When cannabis flower is smoked or vaporized, effects begin within minutes. The first signs are often a tingling or pressure around the eyes and forehead, a warm heaviness in the shoulders, and a noticeably faster heartbeat. The mouth dries ('cottonmouth') and the eyes redden and may feel dry.

Mentally the onset brings a shift in perception: sounds and music can feel richer, thoughts begin to jump and connect in unusual ways, and the mood often lightens into giggliness or relaxed amusement. Some people instead feel a flicker of anxiety as the heart rate rises.

Short-term memory begins to slip early; people lose the thread of a sentence or forget why they walked into a room. Standing up quickly can bring dizziness or, in some first-time users, fainting.

Inhaled THC crosses from the lungs into blood within seconds, and blood levels peak within about 10 minutes, before the high itself peaks, because THC must move from blood into the brain.

Heart rate can rise sharply in the first minutes and blood pressure may drop on standing, which is why some first-time users faint.

### Come-up (5-15 min)

Over 10 to 15 minutes relaxation and laughter build; senses sharpen.

### Peak (15-30 min)

At the peak the body feels relaxed and heavy, sometimes 'couch-locked', especially with sedating strains. Limbs can tingle, and the skin may feel more sensitive. The heart is still fast, and appetite rises sharply ('the munchies').

The emotional tone ranges from euphoric, giggly and sociable to calm and introspective. Time distortion is typical: minutes feel long, and people may lose track of conversations. Colors, tastes and music can feel intensified.

With high-THC products or in vulnerable people, the peak can bring anxiety, paranoia, a racing heart and panic. People with a personal or family history of psychosis face higher risk. Driving is impaired.

At the peak, CB1 activation in the hippocampus and cortex disrupts short-term memory and attention; activation in the cerebellum and basal ganglia slows reaction time, which is why driving is impaired.

Anxiety and paranoia at the peak are linked to THC acting on the amygdala; CBD in the flower may soften this, while high-THC, low-CBD strains make it more likely.

### Plateau (30-60 min)

During the plateau, the body stays relaxed and slightly heavy; the dry mouth and red eyes persist. Hunger often continues.

Mentally there is a mellow, drifting state with slowed time, wandering thoughts and reduced focus. Some feel creative or reflective, others sleepy.

Coordination and memory stay impaired, so driving and risky tasks remain dangerous.

### Comedown (1-3 h)

The comedown is gradual: heaviness turns to tiredness, and many people feel sleepy.

Emotionally people feel calm or slightly flat; some feel foggy for a while.

Regular heavy users may notice irritability, poor sleep and low appetite between uses. Heavy daily use can also cause cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, with cycles of severe vomiting.

As THC moves from blood into fat, effects fade within a few hours even though THC remains in the body for days or weeks in regular users.

In daily users, falling levels may bring irritability and poor sleep; tolerance comes from CB1 receptors reducing in number and partly recovers after about four weeks off.

### After-effects (2-4 h)

Dry mouth, hunger, and a mild fog.

### Next day (next morning)

Some feel slow or foggy the next morning; daily users may feel irritable without it.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses cannabis brings relaxation, mild euphoria, giggliness, and heightened senses. At higher doses, time distortion, anxiety, paranoia, a racing heart, nausea, dizziness and, for some, hallucinations or panic appear. Very high doses, especially edibles, can cause hours of confusion, vomiting and frightening psychotic symptoms.

Stronger flower and repeated inhalation stack quickly; very high-THC flower makes the anxious, paranoid side more likely.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for Cannabis flower (marijuana)

Cannabis alone is not known to cause fatal overdose, but it causes panic, fainting, vomiting, psychosis, and impaired-driving crashes.

Read the shared page: [Cannabis safety: edibles, high-potency products, synthetic cannabinoids](../safety/cannabis-safety.md).
