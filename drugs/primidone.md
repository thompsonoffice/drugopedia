# Primidone (Mysoline)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Barbiturate.

**Brand and street names:** Mysoline.

## History and origins

Approved in 1954; it is turned into phenobarbital in the body.

## Medical use, then and now

Epilepsy and essential tremor; it is not misused in the usual sense.

## How it entered recreational use

Little recreational use; anesthesia drugs are hospital-only.

## Legal status

Prescription-only, not federally controlled in the US.

## What the pills feel like

Mysoline tablets are white and scored.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Primidone's character is drowsiness and unsteadiness, especially in the first weeks.

Like all barbiturates, its safety margin is narrow and alcohol makes it far more dangerous.

Daily use leads to dependence, and withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (1-2 h)

Primidone (Mysoline) is used for epilepsy and essential tremor, and the body converts part of it to phenobarbital. The first doses are known for an unexpectedly strong, unpleasant onset: dizziness, nausea, a spinning or swaying feeling, unsteadiness, and a heavy, drugged sleepiness that can make people feel acutely ill.

Mentally there is fogginess, slowed thinking and sometimes a strange, detached feeling rather than any pleasure.

Tolerance to these first-dose effects develops over days to weeks, which is why doctors start low and increase slowly. Some people take their first doses at bedtime so they sleep through the worst of the dizziness and nausea, and many find the second week far easier than the first.

Medically, the onset reflects how quickly the barbiturate dissolves in fat and crosses into the brain. Primidone is processed mainly by the liver. Short-acting barbiturates enter the brain fast; long-acting phenobarbital is slower.

Early signs are like alcohol intoxication: loosened inhibitions, slurred speech, unsteady walking, and slowed thinking. Judgment fades early, which is part of why accidental overdose was so common in the 20th century.

### Come-up (2-4 h)

Unsteadiness.

### Peak (3-4 h)

At the peak people feel drowsy, unsteady and sometimes nauseated, with double vision or flickering eye movements in some. The body feels heavy and slightly unsteady, and some people notice their tremor quieted almost completely.

Emotionally it is flat, and some people feel low, irritable or emotionally blunted. Many describe feeling 'not quite themselves', with blunted emotions and slower thinking that improves after the first weeks.

Overdose resembles phenobarbital overdose, with deep sedation, slow breathing and low blood pressure. Mixing it with alcohol or other sedatives deepens drowsiness and unsteadiness. Blood tests guide the dose. Vision may blur.

Medically, the peak is when GABA-A channels are held open longest. Unlike benzodiazepines, barbiturates at higher levels can open these channels directly, so there is no ceiling: breathing, blood pressure, and body temperature can all fall, and coma can follow.

The gap between sedation and fatal poisoning is narrow, and alcohol greatly narrows it further. There is no antidote like naloxone; treatment is breathing support in hospital.

### Plateau (many hours)

The plateau is long, with steady drowsiness on regular use. On regular treatment, the body settles into a steady state: mild tiredness, a slight heaviness in the limbs, and sometimes a faint unsteadiness on stairs. For people with essential tremor, hands may be noticeably steadier.

Concentration and memory are dulled, especially early in treatment. Concentration and memory can be mildly dulled, especially early, and some people feel emotionally flat or less motivated until their body adjusts.

Driving should wait until the person knows how it affects them. People are advised to avoid alcohol, and doctors may check blood levels and blood counts during long-term treatment.

### Comedown (1-3 days (phenobarbital))

The comedown is gradual because of the long-lasting phenobarbital it produces. Because of its long-lasting phenobarbital, primidone's effects fade slowly over days rather than hours, so missed doses rarely cause a sudden change, though tremor or seizures may gradually return.

Mood mostly steadies on regular use. Some notice more energy and clearer thinking as levels drop, but also more anxiety or poorer sleep.

Stopping suddenly can trigger seizures, so changes must be made with a doctor. Doctors taper primidone slowly over weeks when it needs to be stopped, both to avoid seizures and to limit barbiturate-like withdrawal such as anxiety, insomnia and shakiness.

As levels fall, the person may remain clumsy and confused for hours, and barbiturates disturb normal sleep stages, so people often wake unrefreshed.

Tolerance to the pleasant effects builds faster than tolerance to the breathing effects, so the danger stays while the high fades. Withdrawal after daily use is among the most dangerous of any drug, with seizures, delirium, and high fever; it needs medical care.

### After-effects (days)

Sedation.

### Next day (still active)

Still active.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, barbiturates cause calm, loosened inhibitions, and a drunk-like feeling. At higher doses, slurred speech, staggering, confusion, and deep sleep follow. As effects keep rising, the person cannot be woken, breathing and blood pressure fall, and body temperature drops: barbiturates can be fatal on their own, and far more easily with alcohol.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

## Overdose note for Primidone (Mysoline)

Overdose causes coma, slowed or stopped breathing, low blood pressure and death; there is no antidote.
