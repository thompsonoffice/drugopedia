# GHB and sodium oxybate (Xyrem, Xywav, Lumryz)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Gamma-hydroxybutyrate: a natural brain chemical and a depressant that acts on GABA-B and GHB receptors. Precursors GBL and 1,4-butanediol turn into GHB in the body.

**Brand and street names:** Xyrem (sodium oxybate), Xywav (mixed salts), Lumryz (once-nightly extended-release). Street: 'G', 'liquid ecstasy', 'Georgia home boy', 'liquid X', 'gina' (GBL).

## History and origins

First synthesized in 1960 by Henri Laborit in France. Sold in US health stores in the 1980s as a bodybuilding supplement until a 1990 FDA ban. It became a party drug and was linked to drug-facilitated sexual assault, leading to the 2000 Hillory J. Farias and Samantha Reid Date-Rape Drug Prohibition Act. Xyrem was approved in 2002 for narcolepsy.

## Medical use, then and now

Narcolepsy with cataplexy and excessive daytime sleepiness (sodium oxybate); used in some countries for alcohol withdrawal.

## How it entered recreational use

Used in clubs and chemsex settings for euphoria, sociability and arousal.

## Legal status

Illicit GHB is US Schedule I; Xyrem and Xywav are Schedule III under a restricted distribution program.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Xyrem and Xywav are taken twice nightly; Lumryz (approved 2023) is an extended-release form taken once at bedtime.

## What the pills feel like

Medical sodium oxybate is a liquid; Lumryz is granules for a suspension. Illicit GHB is a clear liquid that cannot be identified by look or touch.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

GHB's character is alcohol-like euphoria, sociability, warmth and arousal, which can suddenly turn into deep unconsciousness ('going under' or 'G-hole').

The safety margin is extremely narrow and the strength of street liquids varies, so the line between feeling good and being unconscious is small. Vomiting while unconscious is common.

Daily, round-the-clock use leads to severe dependence; GHB withdrawal can cause delirium, hallucinations, seizures and death within hours of the last use, and needs urgent medical care.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (10-20 min)

GHB usually comes on within a short time and is often compared to alcohol, but smoother and quicker. A warm, relaxed glow spreads from the chest outward, muscles loosen, and a pleasant, tingly lightness fills the body. Many describe a mild, wavy dizziness, as if gently rocking, and a softening of the edges of vision.

Mentally the onset is euphoric and disinhibiting. People feel sociable, affectionate, chatty and confident, and many notice heightened touch and sexual interest. Anxiety and self-consciousness melt away, and music and dancing can feel especially good.

The line between this pleasant state and heavy sedation is extremely thin. Small differences in amount, concentration, or adding even a little alcohol can push someone from tipsy to unconscious within minutes, with little warning.

Medically, the onset reflects absorption and entry into the brain. GHB is absorbed quickly and has a half-life of about 30 to 60 minutes, and its absorption is non-linear, so small increases can cause large jumps in effect. Most hypnotics are designed to work quickly so the person falls asleep soon after taking them.

Clinicians advise taking hypnotics only right before bed, because people who stay awake can experience confusion, hallucinations, and memory loss.

### Come-up (15-30 min)

A fast, warm, loosening wave like several drinks at once.

### Peak (30-60 min)

At the peak the body feels warm, loose and heavy, with strong relaxation and a dance-friendly, euphoric buzz. Speech can slur and balance falters, much like being drunk.

Emotionally the peak is open, affectionate, and uninhibited. Many people report heightened touch and closeness. At higher levels, confusion, dizziness and nausea take over, and people may suddenly 'drop', falling into a deep, unrousable sleep, sometimes mid-conversation.

GHB overdose can cause vomiting while unconscious, very slow breathing, seizure-like jerking, slow heart rate and coma. Combining with alcohol is especially dangerous. Anyone who cannot be woken should be placed in the recovery position and 911 called. GHB has also been used in drug-facilitated sexual assault.

Medically, the peak is when sleep pressure is strongest. People who resist sleep at this point may walk, eat, talk, drive, or send messages with no memory afterward ('complex sleep behaviors'), which led to a 2019 FDA boxed warning for zolpidem, zaleplon, and eszopiclone.

Combined with alcohol, opioids, or other sedatives, breathing can slow dangerously, and amnesia is much more likely.

### Plateau (30-90 min)

The plateau is short. As the euphoria peaks and begins to fade, the body grows heavier and drowsier, coordination worsens, and people may sway, slur or yawn repeatedly. Some feel nauseated or dizzy, especially when moving.

Mood stays relaxed and warm, then turns sleepy. Some people feel a pull toward taking more to recapture the high, and timing becomes confusing, which is why re-dosing is a major cause of GHB overdose.

During this phase people can suddenly fall asleep and become unrousable ('G-hole'), sometimes with vomiting or jerking movements. Friends should never leave someone sleeping off GHB alone, and anyone who cannot be woken should be placed on their side and 911 called.

### Comedown (1.5-3 h total)

The comedown can be surprisingly clean for some, who wake feeling refreshed after deep sleep. Others feel tired, foggy, nauseated or headachy, with a heavy body and poor coordination for a few hours.

Emotionally there may be mild anxiety, low mood or embarrassment about gaps in memory. People who use frequently can feel a jittery, anxious rebound within hours of the last dose.

People who use GHB around the clock can develop severe dependence within weeks. Withdrawal can begin within hours and cause intense anxiety, tremor, rapid heart rate, insomnia, delirium, hallucinations and seizures; it can be fatal and needs hospital care. Sudden stopping is never safe.

As levels fall, most people sleep through the comedown. Those who wake early can feel groggy, unsteady, and slow, and next-morning impairment can affect driving, especially in women and older adults.

Nightly use can cause tolerance and rebound insomnia on stopping; some hypnotics (GHB, methaqualone, older drugs) cause severe dependence and dangerous withdrawal.

### After-effects (3-6 h)

Tiredness, sometimes a clear-headed 'reset' feeling, or nausea.

### Next day (usually little)

Little next-day effect unless used repeatedly.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, hypnotics bring sleepiness and calm. At higher doses, or when the person stays awake, confusion, memory loss, hallucinations, and sleep-walking or sleep-driving become likely. With alcohol or opioids, or with drugs like GHB and methaqualone, effects can tip into unconsciousness, vomiting while unconscious, and stopped breathing.

At lower doses GHB feels relaxing and sociable; at higher doses it causes dizziness, confusion, vomiting and sudden unconsciousness, often without warning. The gap is very narrow.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

Not covered: Other routes are not used medically and are not covered.

## Overdose note for GHB and sodium oxybate (Xyrem, Xywav, Lumryz)

Overdose causes sudden unconsciousness, vomiting, slow breathing, slow heartbeat and low body temperature. With alcohol, the risk is much greater. Never leave an unconscious person on their back; use the recovery position and call 911.
