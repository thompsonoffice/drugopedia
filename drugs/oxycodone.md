# Oxycodone

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic opioid made from thebaine (an opium alkaloid); full mu-opioid agonist.

**Brand and street names:** OxyContin, Roxicodone, Oxy IR, Xtampza ER, RoxyBond, Percocet (with acetaminophen), Percodan (with aspirin), Endocet, Roxicet, Tylox (discontinued), Oxaydo; Oxynorm (Europe), Targin/Targiniq (with naloxone).

**Brand and combination product pages:** [Percocet](../products/percocet.md), [Endocet](../products/endocet.md), [Roxicet](../products/roxicet.md), [Percodan](../products/percodan.md), [Combunox](../products/combunox.md), [RoxyBond and Oxaydo](../products/roxybond.md), [OxyContin](../products/oxycontin.md).

## History and origins

Developed in Germany in 1916 by Freund and Speyer at the University of Frankfurt, hoping for a safer alternative to heroin and morphine. Germany marketed it as Eukodal; addiction to it among the elite was called "Eukodalism." Percodan reached the US in 1950 and Percocet in 1976. Purdue Pharma launched OxyContin in 1996 with aggressive marketing that understated addiction risk. Purdue pleaded guilty to federal charges in 2007 and 2020, and OxyContin is widely seen as the spark of the US opioid epidemic.

## Medical use, then and now

Moderate to severe pain, in immediate-release forms for short-term pain and extended-release forms for long-term pain.

## How it entered recreational use

OxyContin, nicknamed "hillbilly heroin", spread quickly in Appalachia and rural America in the late 1990s. In 2010 Purdue reformulated it to resist tampering, which pushed many people toward heroin and later fentanyl. Today, most "oxycodone" pills sold on the street ("M30s", "blues") are counterfeits containing fentanyl.

## Legal status

US Schedule II. UK Class A. Controlled internationally.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Immediate-release oxycodone (Roxicodone, Oxaydo, RoxyBond, generic tablets and capsules, Percocet and other combinations) starts within about 10 to 30 minutes and lasts about 4 to 6 hours, with a noticeable rise, peak, and fall. Extended-release oxycodone (OxyContin, Xtampza ER) is designed to release drug over about 12 hours: it starts more slowly, rises gently, and gives a flatter, steadier effect with little sense of a 'high' when taken as prescribed. Patients describe OxyContin as background pain control rather than a distinct experience, with drowsiness and constipation more noticeable than mood changes. OxyContin was reformulated in 2010 with abuse-deterrent properties. Xtampza ER uses a microsphere-in-capsule design.

**Extra risks of extended-release:** Extended-release products hold far more drug than a single immediate-release tablet, released slowly over many hours. If the release system is damaged, chewed, crushed, dissolved, or taken with alcohol (for some products), the whole reserve can be released at once, which has caused many overdose deaths. ER products are meant only for people with established opioid tolerance and ongoing pain, and they should never be split or shared. Because they last so long, overdose can develop slowly, often during sleep.

## What the pills feel like

Immediate-release oxycodone tablets from US generic makers are mostly small, round, flat tablets with a smooth surface, often with a score line across one side and a debossed (pressed-in) imprint you can sometimes feel. OxyContin extended-release tablets are small, round, bi-convex (domed on both sides), and film-coated, so they feel slick and hard, with no score line; the DailyMed label lists them as debossed 'OP' on one side and the strength on the other. Percocet and its generics are larger, round or oblong tablets, often scored. Oxycodone oral solution is a liquid.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Oxycodone is often described as the most euphoric of the pills: clear-headed, energetic at first, and intensely "feel-good". Compared with morphine, it has less itch and nausea and a brighter, more stimulating rush of well-being. Many people say it makes them chatty, confident, motivated, and affectionate before drowsiness sets in.

That combination of pleasure, functional energy, and relatively few side effects is a big part of why it is so habit-forming. People in recovery often describe oxycodone as the drug that made them feel "normal" or "the best version of themselves" at first, then needed just to avoid withdrawal.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (10-30 min)

The first sign of swallowed immediate-release oxycodone is usually a faint lightness rather than heaviness: a soft warmth that starts behind the breastbone and in the stomach, and a sense that the muscles of the neck and shoulders are quietly letting go. Pain, if present, does not vanish so much as step back, as though it belongs to someone a short distance away. The mouth begins to feel slightly dry.

Mentally, oxycodone's onset is often described as brightening rather than dulling. Mood lifts in a clean, clear way; small worries lose their grip, and people notice they feel more patient, more sociable, and more motivated. Thought stays fairly sharp at this stage, which is part of why many patients do not recognize how strongly the drug is acting.

Small physical signals appear: a mild tickle or itch on the nose or cheeks, a slight warmth or flush in the face, and pupils that start to narrow, making bright light feel less harsh. Some people feel a passing queasiness, especially if they stand up or move quickly, but oxycodone is generally reported to bring less nausea than morphine or codeine.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Oxycodone is metabolized mainly by CYP3A4, with a small part turned into oxymorphone by CYP2D6; half-life about 3 to 4.5 hours for immediate-release. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The come-up is where oxycodone's reputation comes from: a clean rush of well-being, confidence, and energy. People say worries evaporate, they feel affectionate and talkative, and tasks feel easy. Itch is usually mild, often on the nose. Some feel a warm tingling in the limbs.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At the peak, the warmth fills the whole body. Limbs feel pleasantly weighted, as if sinking into whatever they rest on, and skin can feel tingly or prickly with a warm itch, especially on the face, chest and scalp. Breathing becomes slower and shallower, often without any sense of air hunger, and the heart rate may slow slightly. The pupils are pinpoint, and vision can feel soft at the edges.

The emotional tone is the hallmark: a warm, confident, affectionate contentment that people often describe as feeling 'whole' or 'like the best version of myself'. Unlike the heavy dreaminess of morphine, oxycodone's peak tends to feel alert and buoyant at first, with talkativeness and a glowing, uncritical goodwill toward others. Time feels unhurried, and problems seem solvable or unimportant.

This glow hides the danger. Breathing is at its lowest during the peak, and the pleasant feeling gives no warning when oxygen levels fall. In someone without tolerance, or anyone combining oxycodone with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids or sleep medicines, the shift from cozy to unresponsive can happen quietly. Snoring, gurgling, very slow breathing or blue-gray lips are emergency signs.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

As the peak levels off, the bright energy settles into a drowsy, contented stillness. The body feels heavy and warm, eyelids droop, and the head may drop forward and jerk back up, the classic 'nod'. Itching may continue in waves, and people absent-mindedly scratch their nose or arms. The mouth stays dry and urination can feel slow to start.

Mentally the plateau is quieter and more inward. Thoughts drift into loose, half-dreamt images when the eyes close, and conversations may trail off mid-sentence. The emotional tone remains calm and pleasant, but less sparkling; many describe a 'cotton wool' feeling between themselves and the world. Sense of time becomes elastic, with an hour seeming to pass in minutes.

The stomach and bowel are almost still at this point, so fullness, mild bloating and constipation begin. Skin can be flushed and sweaty, especially on the face. Breathing remains depressed, and falling asleep during the plateau, particularly after mixing with other sedatives, is a common setting for overdose.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

The comedown from immediate-release oxycodone is often felt as a fairly sharp drop compared with longer-acting opioids. The warmth recedes from the limbs first, the body begins to feel ordinary or slightly achy, and the itch fades. Ordinary sensations such as noise, light and minor aches seem louder again, and pain being treated returns in the background.

Emotionally, people commonly describe a flat, gray irritability: the warm confidence drains away and leaves restlessness, impatience and low mood. Some describe a short temper sometimes nicknamed 'opiate rage'. This contrast between the glowing peak and the dull aftermath is a strong driver of wanting more, which is a central reason oxycodone is so habit-forming.

Physically, the comedown brings tiredness, a dry mouth, sometimes a mild headache and constipation. In someone with dependence, this phase is where early withdrawal starts: yawning, watery eyes, a runny nose, goosebumps and anxious craving. Taking more at this point stacks on drug still in the body, which is a common path to overdose.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (6-12 h)

After-effects include constipation, dry mouth, and fatigue. Many feel a strong pull to take more to keep the good feeling going, a hallmark of oxycodone's addictiveness.

### Next day

After one use, the next day is mostly normal. After regular use, withdrawal begins within 6 to 12 hours: anxiety, sweating, yawning, goosebumps, aches, insomnia, and intense craving. Tolerance falls by about half within 3 to 7 days without use (PsychonautWiki), which is why relapse after a break is so deadly.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/oxycodone.md): Immediate-release tablets, capsules, liquid; extended-release (OxyContin, Xtampza).
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/oxycodone.md): Misused by snorting, especially immediate-release pills.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/oxycodone.md): Oxycodone injection is used medically in some countries (UK, Australia) but not the US; pills are also misused by injection, which is especially harmful.
- [Rectal (suppositories)](../routes/rectal/oxycodone.md): Oxycodone suppositories (Proladone) are used in Australia.
- [Inhaled and smoked](../routes/inhaled-smoked/oxycodone.md): Misused by smoking pills on foil; counterfeit pills smoked this way often contain fentanyl.

Not covered: No transdermal or sublingual products.

## Overdose note for Oxycodone

Extended-release tablets contain a large reserve; damaging them releases it all at once, and this caused many of the early OxyContin deaths. Counterfeit "blues" and "M30s" commonly contain fentanyl and cause sudden overdose.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
