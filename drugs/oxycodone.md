# Oxycodone

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic opioid made from thebaine (an opium alkaloid); full mu-opioid agonist.

**Brand and street names:** OxyContin, Roxicodone, Oxy IR, Xtampza ER, RoxyBond, Percocet (with acetaminophen), Percodan (with aspirin), Endocet, Roxicet, Tylox (discontinued), Oxaydo; Oxynorm (Europe), Targin/Targiniq (with naloxone).

## History and origins

Developed in Germany in 1916 by Freund and Speyer at the University of Frankfurt, hoping for a safer alternative to heroin and morphine. Germany marketed it as Eukodal; addiction to it among the elite was called "Eukodalism." Percodan reached the US in 1950 and Percocet in 1976. Purdue Pharma launched OxyContin in 1996 with aggressive marketing that understated addiction risk. Purdue pleaded guilty to federal charges in 2007 and 2020, and OxyContin is widely seen as the spark of the US opioid epidemic.

## Medical use, then and now

Moderate to severe pain, in immediate-release forms for short-term pain and extended-release forms for long-term pain.

## How it entered recreational use

OxyContin, nicknamed "hillbilly heroin", spread quickly in Appalachia and rural America in the late 1990s. In 2010 Purdue reformulated it to resist tampering, which pushed many people toward heroin and later fentanyl. Today, most "oxycodone" pills sold on the street ("M30s", "blues") are counterfeits containing fentanyl.

## Legal status

US Schedule II. UK Class A. Controlled internationally.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Oxycodone is often described as the most euphoric of the pills: clear-headed, energetic at first, and intensely "feel-good". Compared with morphine, it has less itch and nausea and a brighter, more stimulating rush of well-being. Many people say it makes them chatty, confident, motivated, and affectionate before drowsiness sets in.

That combination of pleasure, functional energy, and relatively few side effects is a big part of why it is so habit-forming. People in recovery often describe oxycodone as the drug that made them feel "normal" or "the best version of themselves" at first, then needed just to avoid withdrawal.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (10-30 min)

Swallowed immediate-release oxycodone often starts within 10 to 30 minutes. People describe a light, bright warmth in the chest and a lift in mood that comes quickly, with less of the queasy, heavy start of morphine.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Oxycodone is metabolized mainly by CYP3A4, with a small part turned into oxymorphone by CYP2D6; half-life about 3 to 4.5 hours for immediate-release. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The come-up is where oxycodone's reputation comes from: a clean rush of well-being, confidence, and energy. People say worries evaporate, they feel affectionate and talkative, and tasks feel easy. Itch is usually mild, often on the nose. Some feel a warm tingling in the limbs.

### Peak (1-2 h)

The peak, roughly 1 to 2 hours in, is described as intense physical comfort, warmth, love, and bliss, with a clearer head than morphine. People report feeling 'like the best version of themselves', relaxed but not yet sleepy. Breathing slows and pupils pinpoint. This is the most dangerous window for overdose.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

Later, the energy settles into drowsy contentment. Nodding comes, often while sitting up, sometimes with brief dream-like imagery. Sweating and flushing are common.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

The comedown, around 4 to 6 hours for immediate-release, can feel abrupt compared with morphine: the brightness drains away and people describe a flat, irritable, restless feeling. PsychonautWiki notes 'opiate rage', irritability and outbursts, as more common in this phase.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (6-12 h)

After-effects include constipation, dry mouth, and fatigue. Many feel a strong pull to take more to keep the good feeling going, a hallmark of oxycodone's addictiveness.

### Next day

After one use, the next day is mostly normal. After regular use, withdrawal begins within 6 to 12 hours: anxiety, sweating, yawning, goosebumps, aches, insomnia, and intense craving. Tolerance falls by about half within 3 to 7 days without use (PsychonautWiki), which is why relapse after a break is so deadly.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/oxycodone.md): Immediate-release tablets, capsules, liquid; extended-release (OxyContin, Xtampza).
- [Intranasal (nasal sprays and snorting)](../routes/intranasal/oxycodone.md): Misused by snorting, especially immediate-release pills.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/oxycodone.md): Oxycodone injection is used medically in some countries (UK, Australia) but not the US; pills are also misused by injection, which is especially harmful.
- [Rectal (suppositories)](../routes/rectal/oxycodone.md): Oxycodone suppositories (Proladone) are used in Australia.
- [Inhaled and smoked](../routes/inhaled-smoked/oxycodone.md): Misused by smoking pills on foil; counterfeit pills smoked this way often contain fentanyl.

Not covered: No transdermal or sublingual products.

## Overdose note for Oxycodone

Extended-release tablets contain a large reserve; damaging them releases it all at once, and this caused many of the early OxyContin deaths. Counterfeit "blues" and "M30s" commonly contain fentanyl and cause sudden overdose.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
