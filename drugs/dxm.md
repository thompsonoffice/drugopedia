# DXM (dextromethorphan)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Cough suppressant that acts as a dissociative far above medical use.

**Brand and street names:** Robitussin, Delsym, Coricidin HBP Cough and Cold, NyQuil, Mucinex DM; Nuedexta (with quinidine); Auvelity (with bupropion). Street: robo, triple C, skittles, dex.

## History and origins

Approved in the US in 1958 as a non-opioid cough suppressant. 'Robo-tripping' spread among teens in the 1990s and 2000s.

## Medical use, then and now

Cough suppression; with quinidine for pseudobulbar affect; with bupropion for depression since 2022.

## How it entered recreational use

Teens misuse cough products because they are cheap and available; many US states restrict sales to minors.

## Legal status

Over-the-counter in the US; not federally controlled.

## What the products look and feel like

Comes as syrups, gel capsules and small red tablets (Coricidin). Many products also contain acetaminophen or antihistamines.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

DXM's character changes with amount, described by users as 'plateaus': from a drunk, music-enhancing buzz to strong dissociation and loss of body awareness.

It feels 'robotic', with stiff walking, sweating, itching and nausea, and lasts much longer than ketamine.

Many cough products also contain acetaminophen (liver failure), chlorpheniramine (Coricidin: seizures, heart effects) or other drugs, which add serious dangers. DXM with SSRIs or MAOIs can cause serotonin syndrome.

Tolerance to dissociatives builds quickly, and ketamine tolerance leads people to use more, raising the risk of bladder and memory damage.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (30-60 min)

Dextromethorphan (DXM), found in many cough syrups and pills, comes on slowly over an hour or more. Early signs include itching, flushed warm skin, nausea, a slightly drunk wobble and a sense that movements lag behind intention. Walking becomes stiff and mechanical ('robo-walking'), and the body may feel buzzy or numb.

Mentally a dreamy, music-enhanced euphoria develops: songs feel richer and more absorbing, and the person feels pleasantly removed from everyday concerns. At this stage it can feel like being drunk and slightly tripping at once.

Many products also contain acetaminophen, guaifenesin, decongestants or antihistamines, which add serious danger — liver failure, vomiting, heart strain, or delirium.

DXM is absorbed from the gut and converted by CYP2D6 into dextrorphan, the main dissociative compound.

Poor CYP2D6 metabolizers (about 5 to 10 percent of white people) have much higher DXM levels and longer effects.

### Come-up (1-2 h)

A drunk, giddy feeling and heavy limbs build.

### Peak (2-4 h)

At the peak, users describe 'plateaus' that range from mild drunken euphoria to complete dissociation. At deeper levels, the body feels absent or mechanical, vision breaks into frames or tunnels, and hallucinations or dreamlike scenes appear, especially with eyes closed.

Emotionally it is dreamlike, sometimes blissful and expansive, sometimes frightening, confusing or paranoid. Sense of time and identity can be badly disrupted.

Serotonin syndrome with SSRIs or MAOIs, overheating, seizures, and liver failure from combination products are major risks. Dissociatives cause numbness, confusion and loss of coordination, so falls, drowning, burns and traffic injuries are major dangers. Combined with alcohol, opioids or other depressants they can suppress breathing and cause vomiting while unconscious.

At peak, NMDA blockade by dextrorphan causes dissociation, while DXM's serotonin effects add risk of serotonin syndrome with antidepressants.

Coricidin products add chlorpheniramine, an antihistamine that causes delirium, seizures and heart toxicity; acetaminophen-containing products can cause liver failure even days later.

### Plateau (3-6 h)

DXM's plateau is long, several hours, and its character depends heavily on amount. The body often feels heavy, robotic and uncoordinated, with a stiff, mechanical walk ('robo-walk'), itching, sweating, a fast heart and nausea that comes and goes. Many products also contain other drugs, such as acetaminophen or antihistamines, which add their own dangers.

Mentally the plateau ranges from a mild, music-enhancing dreaminess at lower amounts to deep dissociation at higher amounts: a sense of being separated from the body, slowed or looping thoughts, closed-eye dreamscapes and losing track of where one is. Some feel content and reflective; others feel confused or trapped.

Danger rises with serotonin-affecting antidepressants (serotonin syndrome), alcohol, and products containing acetaminophen, which can silently destroy the liver. Overheating, seizures and vomiting while sedated are emergencies.

### Comedown (6-12 h)

The comedown is slow and often unpleasant: a groggy, flat, hungover feeling with headache, fatigue, dizziness and lingering clumsiness, sometimes into the next day. Sleep can be poor. Itching and stomach upset may linger, and the sense of balance can take a while to return.

Emotionally people may feel detached, low, anxious or mentally slowed for a day or more, with a sense that the world is slightly unreal ('afterglow' for some, gloom for others).

Frequent use can lead to dependence, memory problems and depression. Anyone who used a combination cold product should get medical advice about acetaminophen exposure even if they feel fine. Long-term heavy use has been linked to lasting problems with concentration and mood.

DXM's long half-life means effects linger many hours.

Chronic heavy use has been linked to psychosis and, with combination products, liver damage.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Fatigue, low mood and unsteadiness.

### Next day

A hangover-like fog often continues the next day.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses dissociatives bring numbness, lightness, mild euphoria and dreamy detachment. At higher doses, the body feels far away, movement becomes difficult, and vision and sound distort. At still higher levels, people lose contact with surroundings entirely, cannot move or respond, and are at risk of injury, vomiting and choking, with breathing danger when combined with depressants.

Dissociative effects occur only far above the cough-medicine level, and combination products become poisonous from their other ingredients.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, genetics, heart health, mental health history, sleep, heat, hydration, and other drugs (including antidepressants and alcohol) all change the effect. Street products vary hugely in strength and content.

## Overdose note for DXM (dextromethorphan)

DXM poisoning causes agitation, high temperature, fast heart rate, seizures and breathing problems; combination products add acetaminophen liver failure and antihistamine toxicity.
