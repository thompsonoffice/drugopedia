# Phenobarbital (Luminal)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Long-acting barbiturate.

**Brand and street names:** Luminal, Solfoton; Sezaby (IV for newborns, 2022); Donnatal (with belladonna alkaloids). Street: 'phennies', 'purple hearts' (historic).

## History and origins

Synthesized in 1911 and marketed by Bayer as Luminal in 1912. Its anti-seizure effect was found by Alfred Hauptmann in 1912. It is the oldest anti-seizure drug still in wide use and is on the WHO Essential Medicines List.

## Medical use, then and now

Epilepsy (especially in lower-income countries and newborns), status epilepticus, alcohol withdrawal in hospitals.

## How it entered recreational use

Less sought for recreation than short-acting barbiturates because of its slow onset and long duration.

## Legal status

US Schedule IV. UK Class B.

## What the pills feel like

Generic tablets are small, white, and round, often scored.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Phenobarbital's character is slow, heavy, long sedation, more drowsy than euphoric, with dulled thinking and clumsiness that can last into the next day.

Long-term users may feel slowed, forgetful and low in mood.

Its very long half-life means effects and risks pile up over days.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (about 1 h)

Swallowed, it starts slowly, over about 1 hour.

Medically, the onset reflects how quickly the barbiturate dissolves in fat and crosses into the brain. Phenobarbital is partly cleared by the kidneys and partly by the liver, with a half-life of about 2 to 5 days; it speeds up the breakdown of many other medicines. Short-acting barbiturates enter the brain fast; long-acting phenobarbital is slower.

Early signs are like alcohol intoxication: loosened inhibitions, slurred speech, unsteady walking, and slowed thinking. Judgment fades early, which is part of why accidental overdose was so common in the 20th century.

### Come-up (1-3 h)

A heavy, creeping drowsiness.

### Peak (4-12 h)

The peak is reached after several hours, with deep sedation.

Medically, the peak is when GABA-A channels are held open longest. Unlike benzodiazepines, barbiturates at higher levels can open these channels directly, so there is no ceiling: breathing, blood pressure, and body temperature can all fall, and coma can follow.

The gap between sedation and fatal poisoning is narrow, and alcohol greatly narrows it further. There is no antidote like naloxone; treatment is breathing support in hospital.

### Plateau (10-12 h)

Long plateau of many hours.

### Comedown (1-3 days)

It fades over a day or more; its half-life is several days.

As levels fall, the person may remain clumsy and confused for hours, and barbiturates disturb normal sleep stages, so people often wake unrefreshed.

Tolerance to the pleasant effects builds faster than tolerance to the breathing effects, so the danger stays while the high fades. Withdrawal after daily use is among the most dangerous of any drug, with seizures, delirium, and high fever; it needs medical care.

### After-effects (days)

Grogginess, slowed thinking.

### Next day (still active)

Still active for days.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, barbiturates cause calm, loosened inhibitions, and a drunk-like feeling. At higher doses, slurred speech, staggering, confusion, and deep sleep follow. As effects keep rising, the person cannot be woken, breathing and blood pressure fall, and body temperature drops: barbiturates can be fatal on their own, and far more easily with alcohol.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

## Overdose note for Phenobarbital (Luminal)

Overdose causes deep coma, slowed breathing, low blood pressure, low body temperature, and blisters on pressure points; it can last for days.
