# Secobarbital (Seconal)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Mixing depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sleep drugs, muscle relaxants) is the leading cause of deaths with these drugs. Some must never be stopped suddenly after daily use.

## At a glance

Short-acting barbiturate.

**Brand and street names:** Seconal. Street (historic): 'reds', 'red devils', 'seggies'.

## History and origins

Introduced by Eli Lilly in 1934 and widely prescribed for sleep. 'Reds' were heavily misused in the 1960s and 1970s; Seconal was involved in the deaths of Judy Garland (1969) and Jimi Hendrix (1970). Today it is mainly used in medical aid in dying in US states where that is legal.

## Medical use, then and now

Formerly insomnia and preoperative sedation; now mostly medical aid in dying.

## How it entered recreational use

A major street drug of the 1960s and 1970s.

## Legal status

US Schedule II.

## What the pills feel like

Seconal capsules are orange-red.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Secobarbital's character is a fast, strong, drunk-like euphoria and disinhibition, then a heavy slide into sleep.

Users in the 1970s were known for slurred speech, staggering and aggression.

Its narrow safety margin makes overdose easy, especially with alcohol.

Tolerance to the pleasant and sleep effects builds with regular use, but tolerance to slowed breathing builds more slowly, so the danger stays. Daily use can lead to dependence, and stopping suddenly can be dangerous.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (10-15 min)

Secobarbital (the old 'red devils') comes on fairly quickly as a warm, spreading heaviness. The arms and legs feel thick and sluggish, the head sways slightly, and a pleasant flush of warmth moves up the chest into the face, very much like the first strong drink of an evening.

Mentally the onset brings a loosening, disinhibited ease. Self-consciousness drops away, worries feel far off, and people often become talkative, giggly or emotional. Many who have tried both describe it as closer to being drunk than to the cooler, more contained calm of a benzodiazepine.

Coordination slips early: speech slurs, steps become uncertain, and the eyes may flick side to side (nystagmus). Judgment fades before the person feels very impaired.

Medically, the onset reflects how quickly the barbiturate dissolves in fat and crosses into the brain. Secobarbital is processed by the liver with a half-life of about 15 to 40 hours, though the main effect is much shorter. Short-acting barbiturates enter the brain fast; long-acting phenobarbital is slower.

Early signs are like alcohol intoxication: loosened inhibitions, slurred speech, unsteady walking, and slowed thinking. Judgment fades early, which is part of why accidental overdose was so common in the 20th century.

### Come-up (15-30 min)

A rapid wave of looseness and euphoria.

### Peak (about 1 h)

At the peak the body feels heavy, rubbery and numb, as though wrapped in warm blankets. Walking is a stagger, fingers fumble, and the room may sway or spin when the eyes close. Pain and discomfort seem very distant.

Emotionally the peak is a sloppy, carefree euphoria that can swing quickly: laughter turns to tears or to irritable, belligerent moods. Memory begins to fail, and many people later have gaps in what happened.

Barbiturates have a narrow gap between a sedating amount and a fatal one, and unlike benzodiazepines they can stop breathing on their own. Very deep sleep that the person cannot be roused from, slow or irregular breathing, cold clammy skin, or blue lips is an emergency; with alcohol or opioids the danger rises steeply. Naloxone does not reverse barbiturates.

Medically, the peak is when GABA-A channels are held open longest. Unlike benzodiazepines, barbiturates at higher levels can open these channels directly, so there is no ceiling: breathing, blood pressure, and body temperature can all fall, and coma can follow.

The gap between sedation and fatal poisoning is narrow, and alcohol greatly narrows it further. There is no antidote like naloxone; treatment is breathing support in hospital.

### Plateau (1-3 h)

The secobarbital plateau is a deep, drunken drowsiness that slides easily into heavy, almost stunned sleep. People who stay awake look and feel very drunk: speech is thick and slurred, the eyes are glassy and drifting, and walking is a lurching stagger. The body feels numb and distant, and the person may not notice bumps, bruises, cold or the need to use the bathroom.

Mentally the plateau is a fog with emotional weather moving through it. Silliness can flip to weepiness or sudden anger, and people may repeat themselves, argue, or act on impulses they would normally resist. Memory recording is poor, so much of this period is later lost, which is part of why barbiturates were linked to accidents and risky situations in their heyday.

This stretch carries the highest risk of harm from falls, burns, choking on vomit while too sedated to protect the airway, and breathing slowing to a dangerous level, especially if more is taken or alcohol is added. Snoring that turns to gurgling, or breathing that becomes very slow or irregular, is an emergency.

### Comedown (3-4 h main effect)

As secobarbital wears off, a heavy hangover sets in that many compare to a bad alcohol hangover: a dull, pounding headache, dry mouth, aching muscles, nausea and a slow, shaky body. Coordination may stay off for hours, and the eyes may feel heavy and sore.

Emotionally the comedown is often low, irritable and anxious. People may feel shame, confusion or dread about forgotten events, and a jittery, raw restlessness can make it hard to settle, which drives some to take more to smooth it out.

With regular use tolerance builds quickly to the pleasant effects but not to the breathing suppression, so the margin of safety narrows over time. After regular use, barbiturate withdrawal is among the most dangerous of any drug: anxiety, tremor, sweating, delirium, high fever and grand mal seizures can occur, and it can be fatal. Stopping must be medically supervised.

As levels fall, the person may remain clumsy and confused for hours, and barbiturates disturb normal sleep stages, so people often wake unrefreshed.

Tolerance to the pleasant effects builds faster than tolerance to the breathing effects, so the danger stays while the high fades. Withdrawal after daily use is among the most dangerous of any drug, with seizures, delirium, and high fever; it needs medical care.

### After-effects (4-24 h)

Grogginess, poor sleep quality.

### Next day (hangover)

Hangover.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, barbiturates cause calm, loosened inhibitions, and a drunk-like feeling. At higher doses, slurred speech, staggering, confusion, and deep sleep follow. As effects keep rising, the person cannot be woken, breathing and blood pressure fall, and body temperature drops: barbiturates can be fatal on their own, and far more easily with alcohol.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance, body size, age, liver and kidney function, genetics, and especially other depressants (alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, antihistamines) change the effect. Older adults and people with sleep apnea or lung disease are at higher risk. Stronger effects do not stop at more pleasure: they shade into blackouts, falls, and slowed breathing.

Overdose causes coma, stopped breathing and cardiovascular collapse.
