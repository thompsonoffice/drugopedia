# Methadone

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Synthetic opioid; full mu-opioid agonist with a very long, variable half-life, and an NMDA antagonist.

**Brand and street names:** Dolophine, Methadose, Diskets, Physeptone (UK), Metadol (Canada).

## History and origins

Developed in Germany in 1937 to 1939 by Max Bockmühl and Gustav Ehrhart at IG Farben, partly because Germany lacked opium supplies. After the war, the US took the patents. In the 1960s, Vincent Dole, Marie Nyswander, and Mary Jeanne Kreek in New York showed that daily methadone helped people addicted to heroin stabilize their lives, founding methadone maintenance treatment.

## Medical use, then and now

Opioid use disorder treatment, and long-term pain. Its long action means one daily dose prevents withdrawal for a full day.

## Role in addiction treatment

Methadone is one of three FDA-approved medications for opioid use disorder. In the US it can only be dispensed for addiction at certified Opioid Treatment Programs ("methadone clinics"), usually daily at first, with take-home doses earned over time; COVID-era rules allowing more take-homes were made permanent in 2024. Research consistently shows methadone treatment cuts the risk of death roughly in half, reduces HIV and hepatitis C spread, and reduces crime. At a stable treatment dose, people do not feel high; they feel normal, without withdrawal or craving, and can work, drive, and parent. Leaving treatment, especially suddenly, is a high-risk time for overdose because tolerance falls.

## How it entered recreational use

Diverted methadone, particularly pain prescriptions in the 2000s, was linked to a disproportionate share of overdose deaths because people did not expect its slow, long build-up.

## Legal status

US Schedule II. UK Class A.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Methadone has no separate extended-release product; its own long half-life acts like a built-in extended release, which is why it is taken once daily.

## What the pills feel like

Methadone tablets are usually small, round or rectangular, and scored; dispersible tablets (Diskets) are larger, flat, and cross-scored so they can be broken. Clinic methadone is usually a liquid, often colored and flavored, given in a small cup.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Methadone is generally described as the least euphoric of the full agonists: a heavy, warm, calm body feeling, sedation, and relief from withdrawal, rather than a rush. People without tolerance describe it as slow, dull, sleepy, and long; people on treatment describe feeling "normal."

Its effects build up over days of repeated use because it stays in the body so long. Sweating, constipation, and drowsiness are common. People who have quit both heroin and methadone often describe methadone withdrawal as milder but much longer, lasting weeks.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (20-90 min)

Methadone starts slowly, roughly 20 to 90 minutes after swallowing. The first sensations are quiet: a slight warmth, eased tension, and, in someone in withdrawal, the gradual fading of sickness.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Methadone is metabolized by CYP3A4, CYP2B6, and CYP2D6, and its half-life varies enormously, from about 8 to 59 hours, so it accumulates over several days. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (2-4 h)

The come-up is gradual and underwhelming, which is part of the danger. People without tolerance describe a heavy, warm, sedated feeling building over hours, and may wrongly think it is weak.

### Peak (2-6 h)

The felt peak is around 2 to 4 hours, but methadone keeps accumulating, and breathing suppression can deepen after the 'high' has leveled off. People describe a calm, heavy, dull contentment, sweating, and drowsiness.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (6-19 h)

The plateau is very long, often the rest of the day. For people in treatment it feels like being normal: no craving, no sickness, clear enough to work. For people without tolerance it is a long, sedated state.

### Comedown (12-36 h)

Methadone fades over 24 hours or more. There is no sharp comedown; it slowly thins out.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (24-72 h)

Sweating, constipation, low libido, and sleepiness are common ongoing effects. The drug can also change heart rhythm.

### Next day (still active)

Methadone is still active the next day. Daily use builds blood levels for about 5 days before stabilizing, which is why the first week of treatment is the riskiest. Withdrawal starts about 1 to 3 days after stopping and can last weeks.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## How the route changes the experience

- [Oral (swallowed)](../routes/oral/methadone.md): Tablets, dispersible tablets, and liquid concentrate; the standard route for addiction treatment.
- [Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)](../routes/injected/methadone.md): Medical injection exists for patients who cannot swallow; misuse by injection is harmful because of fillers.
- [Rectal (suppositories)](../routes/rectal/methadone.md): Used in palliative care when patients cannot swallow.

Not covered: No transdermal, sublingual, intranasal, or smoked forms.

## Overdose note for Methadone

Methadone overdose is often delayed and long-lasting. Peak breathing suppression can come hours after the peak 'feeling', and deaths often occur during sleep, the night of use or in the first days of starting or increasing. Naloxone wears off far sooner than methadone, so repeated doses and hospital monitoring are needed. Methadone can also cause a dangerous heart rhythm (QT prolongation). Combining with benzodiazepines or alcohol is especially dangerous.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
