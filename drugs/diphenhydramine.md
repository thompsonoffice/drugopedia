# Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) as misused

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Street products are often mislabeled or contaminated, including with fentanyl.

## At a glance

First-generation antihistamine with strong anticholinergic effects.

**Brand and street names:** Benadryl, ZzzQuil, Unisom SleepGels, Tylenol PM and Advil PM (combinations), Sominex. Street: 'the hat man drug'.

## History and origins

Discovered by George Rieveschl in 1943 and approved in 1946 as the first prescription antihistamine; made over-the-counter in the 1980s. A 2020 TikTok 'Benadryl challenge' led to an FDA warning after teen deaths.

## Medical use, then and now

Allergies, hives, motion sickness, sleep aid, and as part of treatment for severe allergic reactions and drug-induced movement disorders.

## How it entered recreational use

Misused at very high amounts for delirium; the experience is almost universally described as frightening.

## Legal status

Over-the-counter; not controlled.

## What the products look and feel like

Pink tablets (Benadryl) or blue gel caps (sleep aids). Check for combination ingredients.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

At medical levels diphenhydramine is simply sedating. Far above that, it becomes a deliriant: confusion, muttering, and realistic hallucinations such as shadow people, insects, or imaginary objects.

People often cannot tell hallucinations from reality, and many describe dark, threatening figures ('the hat man').

It can cause seizures and dangerous heart rhythms, and combination products add acetaminophen or ibuprofen toxicity.

Tolerance develops to some effects, but delirium risk remains.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (30-60 min)

Diphenhydramine (Benadryl and many sleep aids) is a common antihistamine that, in large amounts, acts as a deliriant by blocking the brain's acetylcholine signaling. Taken in large amounts, effects build over an hour or so: a heavy drowsiness, very dry mouth and eyes, flushed, warm skin, blurred vision, and a heart that starts racing. Some people feel restless legs or a creeping unease.

Mentally the onset is foggy and unpleasant for most: confusion, difficulty focusing and a growing dreamlike disorientation, with none of the euphoria people often expect. Some notice shadows moving at the edge of vision.

Large amounts can cause dangerous heart rhythm problems, seizures, very high temperature and death. Social media challenges encouraging diphenhydramine misuse have led to teenage hospitalizations and deaths.

Diphenhydramine is absorbed well and crosses easily into the brain, unlike newer antihistamines.

Blocking histamine H1 receptors causes sedation; blocking muscarinic receptors causes dry mouth, flushing, wide pupils, urinary retention and fast heart rate.

### Come-up (1-2 h)

Dry mouth, fast heartbeat, and confusion at higher amounts.

### Peak (2-4 h)

At the peak the body is hot, flushed and dry, unable to sweat properly, with a very fast heart, wide pupils, blurred vision and trouble urinating. Muscles may twitch or jerk, and walking is unsteady. Speech may be mumbled and hard to understand.

Mentally there is true delirium: people lose track of where they are, talk to people who aren't there, see realistic hallucinations such as insects, spiders, shadow figures or phantom cigarettes, and often believe they are real. Many have no memory of it afterward.

Seizures, heart rhythm problems, very high temperature and agitated behavior can be life-threatening. Poison control or emergency services should be called immediately.

At high levels, muscarinic blockade in the brain causes delirium, and sodium channel blockade in the heart widens the QRS complex, risking fatal arrhythmias.

Seizures are common in severe poisoning; overheating occurs because sweating stops.

### Plateau (4-8 h)

The plateau is long, many hours, with the body staying hot, dry and racing, the bladder full but hard to empty, and the eyes blurred and sensitive to light. Muscles may jerk, and some people pick at the air or their clothes. Constipation and a racing pulse persist, and the person may be unable to sleep despite drowsiness.

Mentally confusion and hallucinations continue, often frightening and repetitive, such as searching for objects or talking on imaginary phones. Fear and paranoia are common. People may not recognize friends or family, and some act out ordinary tasks that aren't happening.

Supportive hospital care, monitoring of the heart and sometimes medicines that reverse anticholinergic effects are needed.

### Comedown (8-12 h)

The comedown can last into the next day or longer, with exhaustion, dry mouth, blurred vision, headache and constipation. Vision may stay blurry for a day or two, and light can feel harsh. Urination may still be difficult. Some feel their heart still racing at rest, and muscles may ache from twitching.

Mentally there is confusion, poor memory, low mood and often a deeply unsettled feeling about what happened. Some feel anxious, scared or shaken for days, and fragments of hallucinations may return as confused memories.

Repeated misuse can lead to dependence and lasting memory problems. Mental-health support is important, especially when misuse is linked to self-harm.

Delirium can outlast the drowsiness by many hours.

Chronic use in older adults is linked to cognitive decline and is on the Beers list of drugs to avoid.

### After-effects (12-24 h)

Grogginess and memory gaps.

### Next day

Next-day fog.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses deliriants cause drowsiness, dry mouth and a fast heartbeat. At higher doses, confusion, blurred vision and hallucinations start. At very high levels, full delirium with realistic hallucinations, overheating, seizures and dangerous heart rhythms occur; no one finds this pleasant.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: body size, tolerance, genetics, mood and setting, health conditions, medicines (especially antidepressants and lithium), and product purity all change the effect.

## Overdose note for Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) as misused

Diphenhydramine overdose causes delirium, seizures, high temperature, dangerous heart rhythms and death.
