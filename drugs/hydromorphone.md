# Hydromorphone

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## At a glance

Semi-synthetic opioid made from morphine; strong full mu-opioid agonist.

**Brand and street names:** Dilaudid, Exalgo (extended-release, discontinued), Palladone (discontinued 2005 after dangerous interactions with alcohol), Hydromorph Contin (Canada).

## History and origins

First made in Germany in 1924 by Knoll and sold as Dilaudid from 1926. Exalgo was approved in 2010. Palladone was withdrawn in 2005 because alcohol could make its capsule release all its drug at once.

## Medical use, then and now

Severe pain, especially in hospitals; given by mouth, injection, pump, and suppository. In British Columbia, Canada, hydromorphone tablets are prescribed in "safer supply" programs as an alternative to the fentanyl-contaminated street supply.

## How it entered recreational use

Known on the street as "dillies" or "D". Popular among people who inject drugs because it dissolves easily and its effect is close to heroin.

## Legal status

US Schedule II. UK Class A.

## Immediate-release vs extended-release

Immediate-release hydromorphone (Dilaudid tablets and liquid) works quickly, often within 15 to 30 minutes, and lasts about 4 hours, with a fast, strong peak. Extended-release hydromorphone (Exalgo, once daily, discontinued in the US; Jurnista and Hydromorph Contin elsewhere) gives a very slow, flat, all-day effect for opioid-tolerant patients. Palladone, an earlier once-daily capsule, was withdrawn in 2005 because alcohol could release all of its drug at once.

**Extra risks of extended-release:** Extended-release products hold far more drug than a single immediate-release tablet, released slowly over many hours. If the release system is damaged, chewed, crushed, dissolved, or taken with alcohol (for some products), the whole reserve can be released at once, which has caused many overdose deaths. ER products are meant only for people with established opioid tolerance and ongoing pain, and they should never be split or shared. Because they last so long, overdose can develop slowly, often during sleep.

## What the pills feel like

Per the DailyMed Dilaudid label, the lower-strength tablets are small, round, and flat-faced with beveled edges and a debossed 'P', while the highest-strength tablet is triangular with a score line between two debossed 'P' letters. Generics vary. Extended-release tablets are film-coated.

**Touch cannot confirm what a pill is.** Different manufacturers make the same medicine in different shapes and sizes, and counterfeit pills, such as fake 'M30' oxycodone 'blues' or fake Xanax 'bars', are pressed to imitate real ones and often contain fentanyl or designer benzodiazepines. Safer ways to check: ask a pharmacist (they can identify a pill from its imprint), use a pill-identifier app or a screen-reader-friendly service such as Be My Eyes or Seeing AI to read the imprint aloud and look it up on DailyMed or Drugs.com, keep medicines in labeled pharmacy bottles, and use fentanyl test strips on anything not from a pharmacy.

## What it feels like

### The character of its effects

Hydromorphone is described as clean, fast, and powerful, with less itch and nausea than morphine. By injection it is famous for an intense rush, often compared to heroin's. By mouth it is shorter-acting and sleepier than oxycodone.

People often describe it as a short, sharp, strong high, which leads to frequent redosing, a pattern that drives dependence.

Tolerance changes everything here. Someone who has never used opioids feels far stronger effects, more nausea, and much higher overdose risk than someone who uses daily, and people who use every day often say they mainly feel 'normal' rather than high.

### Timeline

Times below are typical for the oral (swallowed) route and vary a lot between people. Other routes change the timing; see the route pages.

### Onset (15-30 min)

Hydromorphone (Dilaudid) by mouth comes on fairly quickly with a strong, clean warmth that rises from the stomach into the chest and head. Compared with morphine there is less of a heavy, queasy start; the body loosens quickly and the limbs begin to feel weighted and soft.

Mentally the onset is a fast, intense easing: pain and worry drop away together, and people describe a smooth, bright euphoria that some compare to heroin more than to other pills. Thoughts remain fairly clear at first.

Itch is usually milder than with morphine because hydromorphone releases less histamine, though a tingling flush on the face and scalp is common. Pupils narrow, the mouth dries, and some feel lightheaded on standing.

From a medical point of view, the onset reflects how quickly the drug reaches the brain. Hydromorphone is glucuronidated in the liver; half-life about 2 to 3 hours. As blood levels rise, mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem, limbic system, and spinal cord begin to activate, which is why pain signals soften and mood changes before the body feels fully affected.

Clinicians watch for early signs at this stage: the pupils begin to narrow, breathing rate drops slightly, the face may flush from histamine release, and the gut slows. Patients often report relief of tension or pain before any pleasure, and some feel lightheaded when standing because blood vessels relax.

### Come-up (20-40 min)

The come-up is fast and strong for a pill: a surge of comfort, heaviness, and calm. Some describe a mild 'rush' even when swallowed.

### Peak (30-60 min)

The peak is strong and comparatively short. The whole body feels deeply warm and heavy, with a velvety, sinking relaxation and a pleasant tingle in the skin. Breathing slows noticeably and can become shallow; the heart may slow.

The emotional tone is intense, blissful calm, often described as more euphoric and 'cleaner' than morphine, with fewer side effects in the way. Worries feel entirely irrelevant, and people may become quiet and inward or warmly talkative.

Hydromorphone is very potent, and the peak is when breathing is most suppressed. Hospital patients on it are monitored closely; outside the hospital, mixing with alcohol or benzodiazepines, or use without tolerance, can quickly lead to overdose.

Medically, the peak corresponds to the highest drug levels at the receptors. The same receptors that produce euphoria and pain relief also tell the brainstem to breathe less, so respiratory depression is greatest during the peak. Oxygen levels can fall without the person noticing, because opioids blunt the brain's alarm response to rising carbon dioxide.

Hospital staff monitor sedation and breathing most closely at this point. Signs that the effect is turning dangerous include deepening sleepiness, snoring or gurgling, breathing slower than about one breath every five seconds, and difficulty waking. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or other sedatives makes this phase far more dangerous.

### Plateau (1-3 h)

The plateau is heavy and drowsy, with nodding, slack muscles and a warm, flushed face. Sweating and mild itch come and go.

Mentally people drift between dreamy wakefulness and light sleep, with vivid fragments of imagery and a slowed sense of time. The euphoria softens into contentment.

Constipation, dry mouth and slow urination build. Breathing remains slowed, so sleep during this phase carries risk, especially with other depressants.

### Comedown (3-4 h total)

Because immediate-release hydromorphone is short-acting, the comedown arrives relatively quickly: warmth recedes, the limbs feel ordinary, and pain returns.

Emotionally people describe a noticeable drop into flatness or restlessness, which drives the urge to re-dose.

In dependent people, early withdrawal (yawning, runny nose, sweating, anxiety, craving) can begin within hours. Extended-release Exalgo has a much longer, flatter course.

The comedown reflects falling blood levels as the liver and kidneys clear the drug. Receptor activity decreases, and the brain's normal pain and stress signals return. In people who have used opioids regularly, the nervous system has adapted, so falling levels trigger the beginnings of withdrawal: increased noradrenaline activity causes anxiety, sweating, yawning, and goosebumps.

For long-acting or extended-release forms, the comedown can be very drawn out, and drug can remain active well after the person feels 'normal'. That is one reason taking more during the comedown is a common cause of overdose: the new amount stacks on drug that is still present.

### After-effects (4-12 h)

After-effects include constipation, dry mouth, and sleepiness. Some feel nauseous as it wears off.

### Next day

After regular use, withdrawal sets in within hours with sweats, aches, and cravings.

### Lower vs higher doses (described without amounts)

At lower doses, opioids mainly relieve pain and anxiety, with mild warmth, slight drowsiness, and a gentle lift in mood; many medical patients feel little beyond pain relief. At higher doses, euphoria, heavy body warmth, itching, nausea, and deep sedation become stronger, pupils shrink to pinpoints, and nodding appears. As effects keep rising, nodding turns into being hard to wake, breathing becomes slow, shallow, or irregular, snoring or gurgling begins, and lips turn blue or gray: these are overdose warning signs, and there is often no felt boundary between 'very high' and overdose.

### Why the same amount affects people differently

Why the same amount affects people differently: tolerance is the biggest factor, since daily users may need many times more to feel the same effect, and tolerance drops quickly after a break. Liver enzymes (especially CYP2D6 and CYP3A4) vary by genetics and are changed by other medicines and even grapefruit. Body size, age, kidney and liver health, sleep apnea, and lung disease all matter. Mixing with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives multiplies the effect on breathing. Stronger effects do not stop at a higher level of pleasure: the line between deep sedation and overdose is thin and invisible from the inside.

## Overdose note for Hydromorphone

Very potent compared with morphine, so medication errors confusing the two have killed hospital patients. Extended-release forms with alcohol can release all drug at once.

Read the shared page: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../safety/opioid-overdose.md). Also see [naloxone (Narcan)](../narcan/naloxone.md).
