# Oxycodone: Oral (swallowed)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## About this route

Swallowed as tablets, capsules, or liquids. The drug passes through the gut and liver first (first-pass metabolism), which lowers and slows what reaches the brain. For Oxycodone: Immediate-release tablets, capsules, liquid; extended-release (OxyContin, Xtampza).

## How this route feels different

Swallowing gives the slowest, smoothest, longest experience of the fast routes. There is no "rush"; instead the effects creep in over half an hour or more. Nausea is more common because the drug sits in the stomach. Pain relief lasts longer.

## Timeline for this route

### Onset (10-30 min)

Swallowed immediate-release oxycodone often starts within 10 to 30 minutes. People describe a light, bright warmth in the chest and a lift in mood that comes quickly, with less of the queasy, heavy start of morphine.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The come-up is where oxycodone's reputation comes from: a clean rush of well-being, confidence, and energy. People say worries evaporate, they feel affectionate and talkative, and tasks feel easy.

### Peak (1-2 h)

The peak, roughly 1 to 2 hours in, is described as intense physical comfort, warmth, love, and bliss, with a clearer head than morphine. People report feeling 'like the best version of themselves', relaxed but not yet sleepy.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

Later, the energy settles into drowsy contentment. Nodding comes, often while sitting up, sometimes with brief dream-like imagery.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

The comedown, around 4 to 6 hours for immediate-release, can feel abrupt compared with morphine: the brightness drains away and people describe a flat, irritable, restless feeling. PsychonautWiki notes 'opiate rage', irritability and outbursts, as more common in this phase.

### After-effects (6-12 h)

After-effects include constipation, dry mouth, and fatigue. Many feel a strong pull to take more to keep the good feeling going, a hallmark of oxycodone's addictiveness.

### Next day

After one use, the next day is mostly normal. After regular use, withdrawal begins within 6 to 12 hours: anxiety, sweating, yawning, goosebumps, aches, insomnia, and intense craving.

For the full, detailed description of each phase, see the [general Oxycodone page](../../drugs/oxycodone.md).

## Risks of this route

Slower onset tempts people to take more before the first amount has peaked ("stacking"), a common overdose pattern. Extended-release products hold a large reserve of drug; damaging them releases it all at once and has caused many deaths. Combination products with acetaminophen (paracetamol) can cause liver failure. Constipation and nausea are common.

## Overdose note

Extended-release tablets contain a large reserve; damaging them releases it all at once, and this caused many of the early OxyContin deaths. Counterfeit "blues" and "M30s" commonly contain fentanyl and cause sudden overdose.

## Signs of an opioid overdose

- Breathing that is very slow, shallow, gurgling, or has stopped. Snoring-like sounds can be a warning sign, not sleep.
- Cannot be woken, or does not respond to a loud voice or a firm rub on the breastbone.
- Pinpoint pupils.
- Blue, gray, or ashen lips and fingertips; cold, clammy skin.
- Limp body.

## What to do

- Call 911. Many US states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help.
- Give naloxone (Narcan or another brand) if it is available. It is sold over the counter in US pharmacies. It can wear off before the opioid does, so overdose can return; stay with the person.
- If they are breathing, put them on their side (recovery position) so they do not choke.
- Never leave the person alone to "sleep it off."

Read more: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../../safety/opioid-overdose.md). See also [naloxone (Narcan)](../../narcan/naloxone.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
