# Pentazocine (Talwin): Oral (swallowed)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## About this route

Swallowed as tablets, capsules, or liquids. The drug passes through the gut and liver first (first-pass metabolism), which lowers and slows what reaches the brain. For Pentazocine (Talwin): Tablets.

## How this route feels different

Swallowing gives the slowest, smoothest, longest experience of the fast routes. There is no "rush"; instead the effects creep in over half an hour or more. Nausea is more common because the drug sits in the stomach. Pain relief lasts longer.

## Timeline for this route

### Onset (15-45 min)

Injected, it starts within minutes; nasal spray within about 15 minutes; swallowed within 15 to 30 minutes. Often a light-headed, floaty start.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The come-up brings sleepiness, a 'drunk' or detached feeling, and sometimes dizziness or sweating.

### Peak (1-2 h)

The peak combines pain relief and sedation with dreaminess; some feel anxiety, strange thoughts, or vivid imagery.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

The plateau is sedated and spacey, lasting a few hours.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

It fades over 3 to 6 hours.

### After-effects (6-24 h)

Some feel hungover, dizzy, or low.

### Next day

Next day is usually normal after single use.

For the full, detailed description of each phase, see the [general Pentazocine (Talwin) page](../../drugs/pentazocine.md).

## Risks of this route

Slower onset tempts people to take more before the first amount has peaked ("stacking"), a common overdose pattern. Extended-release products hold a large reserve of drug; damaging them releases it all at once and has caused many deaths. Combination products with acetaminophen (paracetamol) can cause liver failure. Constipation and nausea are common.

## Overdose note

Has a ceiling on breathing suppression alone, but is dangerous with other sedatives. Can cause hallucinations and precipitate withdrawal in people dependent on other opioids.

## Signs of an opioid overdose

- Breathing that is very slow, shallow, gurgling, or has stopped. Snoring-like sounds can be a warning sign, not sleep.
- Cannot be woken, or does not respond to a loud voice or a firm rub on the breastbone.
- Pinpoint pupils.
- Blue, gray, or ashen lips and fingertips; cold, clammy skin.
- Limp body.

## What to do

- Call 911. Many US states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help.
- Give naloxone (Narcan or another brand) if it is available. It is sold over the counter in US pharmacies. It can wear off before the opioid does, so overdose can return; stay with the person.
- If they are breathing, put them on their side (recovery position) so they do not choke.
- Never leave the person alone to "sleep it off."

Read more: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../../safety/opioid-overdose.md). See also [naloxone (Narcan)](../../narcan/naloxone.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
