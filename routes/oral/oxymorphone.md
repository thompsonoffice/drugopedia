# Oxymorphone: Oral (swallowed)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## About this route

Swallowed as tablets, capsules, or liquids. The drug passes through the gut and liver first (first-pass metabolism), which lowers and slows what reaches the brain. For Oxymorphone: Immediate- and extended-release tablets; absorption is poor and food increases it.

## How this route feels different

Swallowing gives the slowest, smoothest, longest experience of the fast routes. There is no "rush"; instead the effects creep in over half an hour or more. Nausea is more common because the drug sits in the stomach. Pain relief lasts longer.

## Timeline for this route

### Onset (15-45 min)

Swallowed oxymorphone can be slow and inconsistent to start, often 30 to 60 minutes, and is strongly affected by food. The first sign is usually a deep, sinking heaviness rather than a bright lift.

### Come-up (30-60 min)

The come-up is heavy and warm: a strong pull toward the couch, flushing, and a powerful wave of comfort. Itch is moderate.

### Peak (1-2 h)

At the peak, oxymorphone is described as one of the strongest pill highs: dense, warm, and euphoric, often compared to heroin. Thinking slows and the body feels like lead.

### Plateau (2-4 h)

The plateau is long and nod-heavy. People report drifting off repeatedly, with dreamlike half-sleep.

### Comedown (4-6 h)

The comedown is slow and sleepy. Many fall asleep as effects fade over 4 to 6 hours for immediate-release.

### After-effects (6-24 h)

After-effects include constipation, sweating, and grogginess. People often describe feeling drained.

### Next day

After regular use, withdrawal is strong and comparable to heroin's: aches, restless legs, sweating, diarrhea, and insomnia.

For the full, detailed description of each phase, see the [general Oxymorphone page](../../drugs/oxymorphone.md).

## Risks of this route

Slower onset tempts people to take more before the first amount has peaked ("stacking"), a common overdose pattern. Extended-release products hold a large reserve of drug; damaging them releases it all at once and has caused many deaths. Combination products with acetaminophen (paracetamol) can cause liver failure. Constipation and nausea are common.

## Overdose note

Taking it with food or alcohol can sharply raise blood levels. The removed Opana ER caused a deadly blood disorder and an HIV outbreak when injected.

## Signs of an opioid overdose

- Breathing that is very slow, shallow, gurgling, or has stopped. Snoring-like sounds can be a warning sign, not sleep.
- Cannot be woken, or does not respond to a loud voice or a firm rub on the breastbone.
- Pinpoint pupils.
- Blue, gray, or ashen lips and fingertips; cold, clammy skin.
- Limp body.

## What to do

- Call 911. Many US states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help.
- Give naloxone (Narcan or another brand) if it is available. It is sold over the counter in US pharmacies. It can wear off before the opioid does, so overdose can return; stay with the person.
- If they are breathing, put them on their side (recovery position) so they do not choke.
- Never leave the person alone to "sleep it off."

Read more: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../../safety/opioid-overdose.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
