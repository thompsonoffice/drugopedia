# Codeine: Injected (intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## About this route

Delivered into a vein, muscle, or under the skin. Intravenous is the fastest and most dangerous route. For Codeine: Codeine injection existed historically and in some countries (IM/SC); rare today.

## How this route feels different

Intravenous use produces the classic 'rush': within seconds a hot, intense wave of pleasure from the stomach up through the chest and head, often compared to orgasm, sometimes with flushing, a heavy body, and a feeling of 'coming home'. It is shorter and the comedown is harder. Intramuscular and subcutaneous injection are slower, without much of a rush.

## Timeline for this route

### Onset (IV seconds; IM/SC 5-15 min)

Codeine starts subtly, often 30 to 60 minutes after swallowing: a slight warmth, relaxation in the shoulders, and frequently an itch on the face, arms, or back that comes before anything pleasant. With cough syrups, the throat feels soothed and the urge to cough fades.

### Come-up (IV under 1 min; IM/SC 10-20 min)

The come-up is gentle and sleepy. People describe a mellow, floaty feeling and mild contentment, like the first glass of wine but heavier in the body.

### Peak (IV 2-10 min; IM/SC 20-60 min)

Codeine's peak, typically 1 to 2 hours in, is a soft, cozy calm with mild euphoria, heavy eyelids, and relaxed muscles, notably less intense than oxycodone or morphine. Itchiness is often the most prominent physical sensation.

### Plateau (30 min-2 h)

The plateau is a lazy, sleepy stretch. People describe sinking into the couch, light nodding, and a preference for quiet music or TV.

### Comedown (2-4 h)

Codeine fades over 4 to 6 hours into tiredness. People often nap.

### After-effects (4-12 h)

Constipation, dry mouth, and a mild headache are common. Combination tablets with acetaminophen add liver strain that is not felt but is real.

### Next day

Most people feel close to normal the next day, maybe slightly foggy. After regular daily use, withdrawal is milder than with stronger opioids but real: restlessness, low mood, aches, runny nose, poor sleep, and diarrhea.

For the full, detailed description of each phase, see the [general Codeine page](../../drugs/codeine.md).

## Risks of this route

The highest overdose risk of any route: the full effect hits within seconds to minutes, with no time to react. Infections (abscesses, endocarditis, sepsis), HIV and hepatitis C from shared equipment, collapsed veins, and damage from pill fillers, which are not made for veins. Syringe service programs offer sterile supplies, testing, and naloxone.

## Overdose note

Ultra-rapid metabolizers can overdose on ordinary amounts. Combination tablets carry a serious risk of liver failure from acetaminophen. Promethazine-codeine syrup combines two sedatives, increasing breathing suppression, particularly with alcohol or benzos.

## Signs of an opioid overdose

- Breathing that is very slow, shallow, gurgling, or has stopped. Snoring-like sounds can be a warning sign, not sleep.
- Cannot be woken, or does not respond to a loud voice or a firm rub on the breastbone.
- Pinpoint pupils.
- Blue, gray, or ashen lips and fingertips; cold, clammy skin.
- Limp body.

## What to do

- Call 911. Many US states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help.
- Give naloxone (Narcan or another brand) if it is available. It is sold over the counter in US pharmacies. It can wear off before the opioid does, so overdose can return; stay with the person.
- If they are breathing, put them on their side (recovery position) so they do not choke.
- Never leave the person alone to "sleep it off."

Read more: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../../safety/opioid-overdose.md). See also [naloxone (Narcan)](../../narcan/naloxone.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
