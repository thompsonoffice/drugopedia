# Morphine: Rectal (suppositories)

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## About this route

Given as suppositories in medicine, mainly when a patient cannot swallow. Absorption is partly protected from first-pass metabolism and varies a lot. For Morphine: Suppositories, mainly in palliative care.

## How this route feels different

Onset sits between swallowing and snorting. It is mostly used medically; experiences vary because absorption is uneven.

## Timeline for this route

### Onset (10-30 min)

Swallowed morphine (immediate-release, typically 10 to 30 minutes) announces itself slowly and often through the stomach first: a vague queasiness or heaviness, then warmth spreading from the belly. Many people notice the itch before any pleasure, typically a prickle at the tip of the nose, the scalp, or the chest.

### Come-up (20-40 min)

Morphine's come-up is a slow tide rather than a wave. Limbs feel weighted and warm, the face flushes, and a drowsy, dreamy contentment builds.

### Peak (45-90 min)

The peak is the classic morphine state: heavy, warm, sleepy bliss, with the body feeling sunk into the bed or chair. Thinking becomes slow and soft, and pain, even severe pain, stops seeming to matter.

### Plateau (1-3 h)

Morphine's plateau is dominated by the nod: drifting into dreamy, half-awake imagery, sometimes vivid and story-like, then surfacing again. People describe losing track of time and conversations.

### Comedown (3-6 h)

As morphine fades, the warmth recedes unevenly: the dreaminess goes first, the heaviness and itch linger. People describe a sleepy, foggy flatness, and many simply fall asleep.

### After-effects (6-24 h)

Constipation is the most persistent after-effect, along with dry mouth and a dull headache. Morphine's metabolite morphine-6-glucuronide is active and builds up if the kidneys are weak, so in some people sedation outlasts the high by many hours.

### Next day

The next day often brings grogginess and a 'hangover' of lethargy. After repeated use, withdrawal begins roughly 8 to 24 hours after the last exposure: yawning, runny nose, watery eyes, aches, chills, diarrhea, and restlessness, with a flu-like peak at 2 to 3 days.

For the full, detailed description of each phase, see the [general Morphine page](../../drugs/morphine.md).

## Risks of this route

Absorption is unpredictable, so the effect can be stronger or weaker than expected. Irritation and tissue injury are possible.

## Overdose note

Morphine and its metabolites build up in people with kidney disease and can cause delayed, prolonged overdose. Extended-release morphine taken in a damaged form releases a full day's supply at once.

## Signs of an opioid overdose

- Breathing that is very slow, shallow, gurgling, or has stopped. Snoring-like sounds can be a warning sign, not sleep.
- Cannot be woken, or does not respond to a loud voice or a firm rub on the breastbone.
- Pinpoint pupils.
- Blue, gray, or ashen lips and fingertips; cold, clammy skin.
- Limp body.

## What to do

- Call 911. Many US states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help.
- Give naloxone (Narcan or another brand) if it is available. It is sold over the counter in US pharmacies. It can wear off before the opioid does, so overdose can return; stay with the person.
- If they are breathing, put them on their side (recovery position) so they do not choke.
- Never leave the person alone to "sleep it off."

Read more: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../../safety/opioid-overdose.md). See also [naloxone (Narcan)](../../narcan/naloxone.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
