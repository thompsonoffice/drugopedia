# Fentanyl (pharmaceutical): Inhaled and smoked

> Educational harm-reduction content. Not medical advice, not a guide to use. Nothing here gives amounts, preparation, or sourcing. Any opioid can cause fatal overdose, and street drugs are frequently contaminated with fentanyl.

## About this route

Vapor or smoke absorbed through the lungs, reaching the brain within seconds. For Fentanyl (pharmaceutical): Nebulized fentanyl is used occasionally in hospitals; smoking is a common illicit route.

## How this route feels different

Almost as fast as injecting, but in shorter, smaller waves with each breath. People describe a quick, warm, dreamy hit that fades fast, leading to frequent redosing.

## Timeline for this route

### Onset (seconds)

In medicine, IV fentanyl acts within seconds. Patients describe a sudden heaviness, warmth, and sometimes a light-headed spin.

### Come-up (seconds)

There is almost no come-up; it arrives all at once, a fast drop into sedation more than a climb into euphoria.

### Peak (1-3 min)

The peak hits within a few minutes: heavy, warm, blank sedation with relief from pain. Breathing can slow or stop quickly, and chest muscles can stiffen.

### Plateau (5-20 min)

The plateau is short, often 30 to 60 minutes after an IV dose, with deep nodding or sleep.

### Comedown (20-60 min)

Fentanyl wears off quickly, and the return is often abrupt, with restless discomfort.

### After-effects (1-4 h)

From patches, effects persist for many hours after removal because drug remains in the skin.

### Next day

After repeated use, withdrawal comes quickly and is intense.

For the full, detailed description of each phase, see the [general Fentanyl (pharmaceutical) page](../../drugs/fentanyl.md).

## Risks of this route

Very fast onset makes the drug feel compulsive, encouraging repeated use and dependence. Overdose can happen within minutes. Lung irritation, burns, and shared equipment risks. Smoking is lower risk than injecting for infections, but is not safe.

## Overdose note

Fentanyl overdose can happen within seconds to minutes. It can cause 'wooden chest' (chest-wall rigidity), where the breathing muscles go stiff. More than one naloxone dose may be needed. Heat on a patch speeds release.

## Signs of an opioid overdose

- Breathing that is very slow, shallow, gurgling, or has stopped. Snoring-like sounds can be a warning sign, not sleep.
- Cannot be woken, or does not respond to a loud voice or a firm rub on the breastbone.
- Pinpoint pupils.
- Blue, gray, or ashen lips and fingertips; cold, clammy skin.
- Limp body.

## What to do

- Call 911. Many US states have Good Samaritan laws that protect people who call for help.
- Give naloxone (Narcan or another brand) if it is available. It is sold over the counter in US pharmacies. It can wear off before the opioid does, so overdose can return; stay with the person.
- If they are breathing, put them on their side (recovery position) so they do not choke.
- Never leave the person alone to "sleep it off."

Read more: [What an opioid overdose feels and looks like, and what to do](../../safety/opioid-overdose.md).

## Getting help

SAMHSA National Helpline: **1-800-662-4357** (free, confidential, 24/7, English and Spanish). In an emergency, call **911**.
